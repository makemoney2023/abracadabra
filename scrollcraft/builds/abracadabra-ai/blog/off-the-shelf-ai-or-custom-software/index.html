Off-the-Shelf AI or Custom Software

Buy the product when it already does the job. Build only when the job is yours alone. Surveys show generative AI use is already common among small firms.

By Christopher Buzny · 2026-10-14

Off-the-shelf AI is the right buy when a product already does the job. Custom software is the right build when the job is specific to how this business works and no product covers it. We start there because owners usually start at the opposite end. They hear that serious firms build, or they hear that nobody should build, and both sermons skip the only question that matters. Does a product already do this, with a person you can name on the output? If yes, buy it. If the job depends on your records, your exceptions, and your checker, the product will not invent those. That is the custom case, and it is rarer than the sales conversation suggests.

The studio takes the work that products do not cover. The consultant note is what to ask before either path. The agency comparison is who you hire once you know you're building, or once you know you shouldn't. We'd rather lose a build than construct a chassis for a job a sealed box already does. The box is not an insult. The box is how a small firm stays a small firm instead of accidentally becoming a software company.

What the surveys count

The Chamber's 2025 report says 58% of small businesses use generative AI, up from 40% in 2024 and 23% in 2023.1 That is a use claim. It is not a claim that those firms built custom software. Use can be a tab someone opened. Use can be a draft inside a product they already pay for. The rise across those years says owners are trying the tools. It doesn't say they hired anyone to pour a foundation. When a pitch borrows 58% and then offers you a custom build, it has changed the subject. The subject was use. The invoice is for a build. Those are not the same purchase.

Salesforce surveyed 3,350 leaders at firms of 200 employees and under.2 75% were at least experimenting. 83% of growing small and midsize businesses said that. 91% of those already using AI said it boosts revenue. The survey does not say whether the tool was bought or built. Read that last sentence twice before you let a revenue line choose your architecture. "At least experimenting" is a wide door. Growing firms saying the same thing is still a wide door. A revenue boost, among those already using AI, doesn't identify the vendor and doesn't identify a codebase you will have to feed. We've heard the revenue line used as a reason to build. The line doesn't know what was bought.

Run the Readiness Check

If a bought product covers the job, buy it. If the job depends on your records, your exceptions, and your checker, the product will not invent those. That is the custom case. Records means the files you actually keep, not the files a demo assumed. Exceptions mean the customer you treat differently and the job you refuse. The checker means a named person who can say the output is wrong. Miss any of the three and you don't have a custom case. You have a wish that someone will design your business for you while they design the software.

A worked example

Take a shop whose reorder already lives in the register. This is a walkthrough, not a result. The owner gets a flag when a staple item runs low. The Saturday clerk trusts the flag enough to pull a box. The bookkeeper doesn't need a second opinion unless the flag is about a special order. A cousin in another trade told the owner to "build something with AI" because the cousin saw a demo. Building here would mean a second list that drifts from the register, a login the clerk won't open on a busy Saturday, and a custom pile that still doesn't know the staple from the special order any better than the flag does. Buy, or rather keep, the product. Spend the attention on the exception the flag gets wrong, and write down who decides that exception. That's the whole project. It might be a setting. It might be a sentence on the wall. It is not a chassis.

A trades desk is where we stop saying buy so quickly. The quote starts as a voicemail, becomes a site note, picks up photos from the lead tech's phone, and waits for the owner to set a price. The office manager sends it only after the price is there. No product they already pay for holds that stack. The accounting tool holds the invoice after the yes. The inbox holds the chaos before it. A bought "AI assistant" can draft a friendly paragraph and still lose the measurement. Custom, in this room, means a path that keeps the measurement, the photo, and the price-shaped hole together until the owner fills the hole. The price stays a hole on purpose. We will not build a system that invents it. If a vendor says their shelf product already does the whole path, make them do yesterday's quote in front of you. If they can, buy. If they redraw your job until it fits their screens, you're back in the custom case, or you're in the case where you change your job to fit a product. Changing the job is allowed. Pretending you didn't is how the build gets blamed later.

A clinic usually looks like the shop until someone describes intake. Plain reminders are a product. Many booking tools already send them. Don't commission a reminder engine so the waiting room can say you built something. The custom candidate is the weird intake: the practitioner's rules about which visits are in scope, the note billing needs, the patient who must be called rather than messaged. If those rules live only in the practitioner's head, a product will not invent them, and a custom build will only freeze a bad guess. Write the rules first. Then see if a product has a place to put them. Only the leftover is a build. We've watched clinics skip to the leftover and then discover the booking tool had a field for it the whole time. The field isn't glamorous. Glamour is a poor reason to hire a developer.

How we'd run it

First, write the job as a single sentence with a person on the output. The clerk acts on the reorder flag. The owner sets the price after the note is complete. The front desk sends the reminder after reading it. If the sentence has no person, you don't know what you're buying or building. You're shopping for a vibe.

Second, list the products you already pay for and ask, in writing, whether any of them do that sentence. Ask the person who uses the product, not only the person who receives the invoice. The bookkeeper knows whether late invoices are already flagged. The clerk knows whether the reorder flag is a lie. The front desk knows whether reminders already go out. A consultant is useful here when the owner is too close to the demo to see the product they have.

Third, try the shelf product on yesterday's real job before you talk to a builder. Not a sample record. Yesterday's awkward one. The special order. The quote with the missing measurement. The patient who has to be called. If the product survives the awkward one with a person checking it, stop. You have your answer. Custom work that begins before this trial is how you pay to rediscover a checkbox.

Fourth, if the product fails, write down the exact miss. "It can't hold the site photo with the measurement." "It has no place for the practitioner's scope rule." That sentence is the spec. A builder who won't repeat the sentence back is about to build a different job, one their framework likes. The agency comparison matters only after this sentence exists. Before it, an agency is a guess with a team.

Fifth, name the checker in the thing you buy and in the thing you build. A shelf tool without a person is still a risk. A custom tool without a person is a more expensive risk, because you now own the repairs. The revenue comment in the Salesforce survey is not a substitute for that name. A boost, among people already using AI, doesn't tell you who presses send.

Sixth, decide what you will not maintain. Custom software is a relationship with your own exceptions. If nobody on the desk can describe the exception a year from now, don't encode it. Leave it as a human step. The cheapest system is the one that refuses to swallow a judgment. We like that cheapness. It keeps the owner in the business they meant to run.

Common mistakes

Hearing "most small businesses use AI" and translating it into "most small businesses built their own" is the mistake the Chamber figure keeps feeding. 58% is use. Use went up from the prior years in that report. Use is a login, a draft, a feature inside something you already rent. We've had to say this in rooms where a relative had already told the owner that renting was naive. Renting a product that does the job is the grown-up move. Building a second version of the register is the naive one, and it wears a very serious face.

Letting the revenue line pick the architecture is the next one. 91% of those already using AI said it boosts revenue. That's a report about users, not a promise about your chassis. It doesn't split bought from built. A shop can see a lift from a better reminder inside a tool they have, and a trades desk can see nothing from a custom portal that the office manager resents. The survey can't see your resentment. You can. 75% at least experimenting, and 83% of growing firms saying that, means a lot of people are trying things. Trying is not a blueprint.

Building the solved job because custom feels like ownership is how small firms acquire a second business they didn't want. Ownership of code you don't have time to feed is a liability that looks like an asset in the proposal. The proposal shows a tidy chassis. The Tuesday after launch shows a clerk who still uses the register, plus a system that pages you when a field is empty. You will maintain both. The cousin who recommended the build will not.

Skipping yesterday's awkward job is how the trial lies. Teams test the happy path. The happy path fits every product. Your business is the exception: the refund you allow, the visit you refuse, the price you won't put in an email. If the trial never touches those, you learned nothing. You watched a demo with your logo near it. Demand the awkward one. If a seller won't run it, believe them. They told you the product doesn't cover the job.

Freezing unwritten rules into custom software is the mistake that feels like progress. The practitioner's scope rules aren't on a page. The builder interviews them for an hour and encodes the hour. Next month a case shows up that the hour didn't cover. The software is now confidently wrong, and changing it is a project. A product with a blank field would have been more honest. Write the rule until a new hire could apply it. Then decide if it needs software at all.

What we'd do

We'd buy first, and we'd be stubborn about it. That's the opinion. The default for a shop, a clinic, and most trades desks is the product that already does the sentence, with a named person on the output. Custom is a leftover, not a personality. We don't think building is a sign of ambition. We think building is what you do when the leftover is real and you can point at it without waving.

We'd make any builder repeat the miss before they estimate. We'd make any shelf vendor survive yesterday's awkward job. We'd keep the price, the clinical scope, and the exception for a regular customer in human hands unless a product has a clear place to hold the rule and a person still checks the result. We wouldn't let 58% or a revenue percentage choose. Those numbers count use and reported boosts. They don't count your records.

If you're staring at a proposal this week, put it down and open the tool you already pay for. Try the job once, on purpose, with the person who will live with the result sitting next to you. If it works, you're done, and done is a good outcome. If it fails, you'll have the sentence the custom work has to honor. Either way you won't have bought a chassis to impress a chart.

Questions

When should you buy off the shelf?

When a product already does the job and you can name who checks the output. A custom build for a solved job wastes the budget.

When is custom software the right path?

When the job depends on records and exceptions a product does not cover, and you can still name the checker.

Does generative AI use mean firms built their own tools?

No. The Chamber's 2025 figure, 58%, counts use. It does not count custom builds.
