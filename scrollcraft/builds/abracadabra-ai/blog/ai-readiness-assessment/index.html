AI Readiness Assessment: What It Measures and How to Score

An AI readiness assessment scores data, process, people, and decisions. Compare public tools, then read what each Readiness Check band means.

By Christopher Buzny · 2026-10-14

An AI readiness assessment asks whether a business can put a tool to work. Public indexes and checklists answer that question for different audiences, and they are easy to mix up. This note compares them, then states what the Readiness Check guide scores for the firm actually in the room.

We get asked for "our number," as if readiness were a credit score you could borrow from a headline. A figure about senior leaders at large companies does not tell a clinic whether the front desk and the clinician share one record.

What the public tools measure

Cisco's third annual AI Readiness Index, released 14 October 2025, surveyed 8,000 senior leaders in organizations with more than 500 employees, across 30 markets and 26 industries.1 Pacesetters stayed near 13% for three years. Those organizations were 4 times more likely to move a pilot into production, and 50% more likely to see measurable value. 83% of respondents plan to deploy AI agents, and nearly 40% expect agents to work alongside employees within a year. That sample is not a small-business survey.

Owners hear the Cisco line on a podcast and want to know if they are behind. Behind what? Behind a company large enough to put a senior leader on a survey. Pacesetters near 13% of that sample, held for three years, is a humility check for big firms. It is a bad mirror for a practice with a front desk and a couple of treatment rooms. The part that still rhymes is the production gap. The organizations that move a pilot into the real work are the ones with a written job. The demo was never the hard part. If you use Cisco at all in a small firm, use it as a warning against mistaking a pilot for a habit, not as a ranking you are supposed to join.

Gartner reported on 26 February 2025 that 63% of respondents do not have, or are unsure they have, the data management practices AI needs.2 The same note predicts that through 2026 organizations will abandon 60% of AI projects that lack AI-ready data. That is the line we underline when someone wants to skip the record and start with a login. The project stops because the data was never ready. There is no mystery ending.

The OECD SME AI adoption tool, part of the 2026 French Group of Seven Presidency work, is a short self-check for smaller firms.3 It takes about five minutes, stores no data, and keeps answers in the browser. As of May 2026 it is a pilot, with preliminary validation by Group of Seven governments. It does not publish an adoption rate. We like it as questions you can answer without creating an account. We do not like it as a stand-in for a national share, because it refuses to be one. If a tool will not give you a rate, do not invent one after the meeting.

AWS published an AI readiness checklist in September 2025.4 It walks five steps and sorts a business into four stages: Not yet ready, Emerging, Operational, and Scaling. The stages cover data, the use case, the stack, governance, and the metrics you will watch. The page does not cite an adoption percentage. Stages are descriptions you can argue with. A percentage nobody counted is a rumor with a percent sign.

The Readiness Check is the one built for the firm in the room. It educates and routes. It does not diagnose. Christopher Buzny publishes the method on the studio site.

We draw that line on purpose. A diagnosis says what is wrong with you. A route says where to look next. Owners sometimes want the diagnosis because it sounds more serious. Serious is not the same as useful. If we told a clinic it had failed readiness, we would be borrowing a clinical tone for a business conversation. The check describes a state and points at a next page. That is the promise, and we would not widen it.

What each band means

The check scores from 0 to 100. Bands are Early (0–39), Forming (40–59), Ready (60–79), and Running (80–100).5

  • Early: The work that matters still lives in people's heads. The first useful move is to write one workflow down and name who owns it.
  • Forming: Pieces of the work are written down. A named owner and one system of record are what turn that into something a team can run.
  • Ready: The business can put a new system to work. The open question is which workflow to start with, and who owns it day to day.
  • Running: A real workflow already uses the tools. The next gain is the job that still waits on one person.

A band is a state, not a grade. The four scores behind it are data, process, people, and the decision. The check takes five to seven minutes, one question per screen, and returns three suggestions for each section.

Say the bands in speech, not as a ladder to climb. Early is heads, not systems, so you write one workflow and name the owner before you shop. Forming is partial writing. The missing pieces are a named owner and one system of record, and without those the team cannot run the job when the usual person is out. Ready means a new system can land. The argument left is which workflow goes first, and who lives with it on an ordinary day. Running means a real workflow already uses the tools. The gain left is the job that still waits on one person, the one everyone routes around because it is "easier to ask her."

People treat Running as the compliment and Early as the scolding. We do not. Early is a clear description, and it is often the honest one. A clinic can be excellent at care and Early at the paperwork around recalls. Those are different skills. The band is about whether a tool has anything solid to stand on. It is not a review of the owner's character.

The next note looks at how surveys count AI in small businesses, which is a different question from whether one firm is ready. A survey can talk about firms in general. It cannot say your front desk and your clinician agree on the chart note. Do not let the wide question answer the narrow one.

A worked example

Say we are in a small physiotherapy clinic after the last patient. Priya owns the practice. Sam runs the front desk. Andre is the clinician who sees most of the repeat visits. This is an example we walk through, not a clinic we measured.

Priya has been shown a tool that would draft recall messages and summarize visit notes. She wants to know if the practice is "ready" before she signs. We refuse to start with the tool. We start with the four scores, in words, the way the check will later put them in a band.

Data, for the recall job, is a mess in the ordinary sense. Sam has a list in the booking screen. Andre keeps a notebook of people he meant to see again. Priya trusts a spreadsheet she built when the booking screen lost a column. Asked which list is true, they look at each other. That is not a failure of care. It is an absent source of truth. We would not call data "in place" while three lists can disagree and nobody is embarrassed.

Process is partial. The happy path is spoken: Andre finishes, Sam books the next visit, the patient leaves with a card. The exception is not spoken. What if the patient will not book today? What if Andre wants a recall in six weeks but Sam only sees open slots this month? What if the note is not finished before the patient reaches the desk? Priya says they "work it out." Working it out is Forming at best. It is not a process a tool can follow without inventing the rest.

People are the surprise, same as in most rooms. Sam already pastes awkward wording into a personal chat tool so the recall text sounds less cold. Andre did not know. Priya did not know. Nobody has said whether those words may go to a patient. The people score is not about enthusiasm. It is about permission.

The decision is a blank. Priya says success would be "fewer no-shows" and then cannot say who would turn the tool off if a message went to the wrong person. Until that sentence exists, we will not talk about agents, pilots, or pacesetters. Cisco's large-firm picture is interesting and irrelevant to whether Sam and Andre share a list.

If we scored this room in the check's language, we would expect Early or Forming, and we would say so without flinching. The work that matters for this job still lives in heads and side lists. The useful move is to write the recall workflow down and name who owns it. That is the Early sentence, and it fits.

How we'd run it

First we name the job. One job. At the clinic it is the recall, from Andre deciding someone should come back to the patient actually having a time. If Priya says "everything," we shrink it until it has a start and a finish.

Second we pull the public tools apart so nobody borrows the wrong population. Cisco describes organizations with more than 500 employees. The OECD tool is a short self-check that stays in the browser and publishes no rate. AWS offers stage names and does not cite an adoption percentage. None of those is a score for this clinic. We say that before anyone feels behind.

Third we score data, process, people, and the decision in words, using absent, partial, in place, or running only if we can point. Sam, Andre, and Priya each answer. Disagreement is the finding. We write it down instead of averaging it into a mood.

Fourth we take the check if they want the band. Five to seven minutes, one question per screen, three suggestions per section. We read Early, Forming, Ready, or Running as a state. We do not add a pep talk that turns Early into "almost Ready."

Fifth we pick one next page, not five projects. If the record is the blank, we fix the list Sam and Andre will both believe. If the writing is the blank, we write the exception for the patient who will not book today. The tool demo can wait until that page exists.

Common mistakes

The mistake we see most is treating Cisco's pacesetters as a small-business grade. Near 13% of a large-firm sample, 4 times as likely to move a pilot into production, is about those firms. Quoting it at a clinic as "only the top shops are ready" invents a ranking the study did not make.

Another mistake is averaging the four scores into a feeling. Data can be absent while people are eager. Eagerness is not a score. The band names a state, and the useful part is the weak state, not the average comfort in the room.

A third mistake is using the OECD tool's silence as a zero. It does not publish an adoption rate. Silence is not a finding that small firms are unready. It is a tool that refused to pretend.

We also watch owners skip from a band to a purchase in the same sitting. Ready still asks which workflow to start with and who owns it day to day. The band did not pick the vendor. You still have to.

What we'd do

We would score the clinic in front of us and leave Cisco in the footnote where it belongs. Our opinion: a small practice does not need to know whether it is a pacesetter. It needs to know whether Sam's list and Andre's notebook are the same list. Until they are, a recall tool will write confident messages from a record nobody owns.

If we were Priya, we would spend the next ordinary weeks on one written recall path, including the patient who will not book at the desk, and we would tell Sam to stop sending patient wording from a personal account until Priya has said what may go out. Then we would take the check again and read the band as a state, not as a prize. If it is still Early, we would believe it.

Questions

What does an AI readiness assessment measure?

It names whether the data, the process, the people, and the decision are in a state where a tool can do a job. Public tools measure different populations. The Readiness Check scores the business taking it.

Is the Cisco index a small-business score?

No. The 2025 index surveyed senior leaders at organizations with more than 500 employees. It is a useful comparison for large firms, and it is the wrong sample for a small business.

What do the Readiness Check bands mean?

Early is 0–39, Forming is 40–59, Ready is 60–79, and Running is 80–100. Each sentence on the check describes a state. The check does not diagnose.
