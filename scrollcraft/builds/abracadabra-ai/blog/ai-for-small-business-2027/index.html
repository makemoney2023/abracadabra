AI for Small Business: What the Surveys Still Say

Two surveys still disagree because they ask different questions. One counts generative AI use. The other counts AI used to produce the goods or the service.

By Christopher Buzny · 2026-10-14

The useful question for a small business is which job actually changes. Two surveys from 2025 are still the clean public counts, and they do not measure the same thing. One asks whether a small business uses generative AI. The other asks whether a business used AI to produce the goods or deliver the service. You can say yes to the first and no to the second and be honest both times. Averaging them into a single "how behind are we" number is how owners buy the wrong project.

We wrote the full split in the help note. What to do with the gap is the strategy note. How to score one firm, instead of scoring a country, is the assessment. A survey is a crowd. Your shop is one set of hands, one set of files, and one job that is slow this week. Start there.

We get asked which percentage to believe. We do not believe either one as a verdict on a single owner. We believe the wording of the question. If you skip the wording, you will spend a season chasing a rank that was never about your counter.

Two questions, two answers

The Chamber's 2025 report says 58% of small businesses use generative AI, up from 40% in 2024 and 23% in 2023.1 That question is about use of a generative tool. Writing a product blurb, drafting a reply, sketching a social post, asking for a checklist. It counts the owner who opened the tool on a Tuesday and got something usable enough to edit. It does not ask whether the goods on the shelf were produced by a model, or whether the service the customer paid for was delivered by one.

Statistics Canada, for 2025, found 12.2% of businesses used AI to produce goods or deliver services, up from 6.1% a year earlier.2 That is a harder, narrower question. It is about the thing you sell. A bakery that uses a tool to write the weekly email can sit inside the Chamber figure and outside the Statistics Canada figure. A shop that lets a model propose purchase orders, which a person then places, is edging toward the second question and still may not meet it. Read the verb. Produce. Deliver. Not draft. Not brainstorm. Not "we tried it."

A survey is not this business. Run the Readiness Check.

Run the Readiness Check

A firm can say yes to the Chamber question and no to the Statistics Canada question. Plan from the job you can name. Do not average the two percentages into a single readiness rate. This site does not publish an aggregate score from the Readiness Check. If someone offers you a blended "small business AI adoption" figure made from these two, they have glued different questions together and called the glue insight.

A worked example

Walk a home-goods shop with us. This is an example, not a study, and we are not attaching a score to it. The owner buys from a few makers and from one wholesaler. A floor lead works the counter and knows which lamp has a chip in the glaze. A bookkeeper comes in part of the week and hates surprises in the inbox. There is no IT person. There is a group text.

Last winter the owner started using a generative tool for product descriptions. She pastes a few notes from the maker, asks for a short paragraph in her voice, and edits out anything that sounds like a catalog from nowhere. The floor lead does the same for a social caption when a new table arrives, then deletes the line that claims the wood is oak when it is not. That is real use. On the Chamber question, this shop says yes. Generative AI is in the week.

Nothing about that use produces the lamp or delivers the sale. The owner still chooses the maker. The floor lead still wraps the lamp, still tells the truth about the chip, still calls the customer when the freight is late. The bookkeeper still matches the invoice to the packing slip with her eyes. On the Statistics Canada question, this shop says no. AI is not producing the goods and it is not delivering the service. Both answers are correct. A consultant who collapses them into "you are an AI business now" or "you are behind the market" has not listened.

Here is the job that might move them toward the second question, if they ever want that. The wholesaler's invoices arrive as scans with line items the bookkeeper retypes. A tool that reads the scan and proposes the lines, with the bookkeeper still posting them, is closer to delivery of the back-office service than a prettier lamp description is. It is still not the shop "running on AI." It is one job, with a checker, touching the thing they already do to get paid. We would talk about that job for an hour before we ever mentioned the Chamber share.

The trap in this shop is pride in the wrong direction. The owner feels modern because the descriptions improved, and a nephew told her that most small businesses are already doing this. He was waving at the Chamber figure without saying the question. She also feels secretly behind because a podcast said only a small slice of firms use AI in production. That podcast was waving at a figure like the Statistics Canada one, again without the question. She is neither a laggard nor finished. She has a drafting habit and an untouched invoice pile.

How we'd run it

We do not start by asking an owner where they rank. We start by forcing the two questions apart, then we pick a job.

First, we ask what they already open in a week. The shop owner will say the writing tool. We write that down as generative use, in the Chamber sense, and we do not praise it as a strategy. We also do not scold it. A decent product description that a human edits is a fair use of an afternoon. It is just not the same fact as AI inside delivery.

Second, we ask what the customer actually pays for, and what has to be true for the money to be earned. In the shop, that is the right object, in the condition the floor lead described, handed over or shipped, with the books able to explain the invoice. We look for any step in that chain where a model already acts. Usually there is none. We say so. The Statistics Canada question stays a no, and the no is useful. It stops the owner from advertising a capability she does not have.

Third, we pick one candidate job that could change the chain, and we describe it in a sentence a new hire could follow. For this shop we like the scanned invoice, not the caption. The sentence is: read the wholesaler scan, propose the lines, and wait for the bookkeeper to post. If the owner wants the caption "automated" instead, we will do it only after she admits it does not change delivery. Sometimes that is still worth a small habit. It is not worth a project with a name.

Fourth, we name the checker and the failure. The bookkeeper is the checker. Failure is a line that does not match the packing slip, or a vendor she does not recognize. If she cannot see the original scan beside the proposal, the job is not safe, and we do not turn it on. This is the same discipline as any other install. The survey cannot do it for her.

Fifth, we refuse the blended score. We will not tell this owner she is at some midpoint between the Chamber's use figure and Statistics Canada's production figure. Those populations and those verbs are different. The assessment scores her files, her people, and her job. The strategy note is where the one-page plan goes after the job is chosen. The help note is the longer version of the split if a partner still wants to argue from a headline.

Common mistakes

Quoting a single percentage in a staff meeting is the fastest way to start a useless argument. "Most small businesses already use it" points at the Chamber's 58% in the 2025 report, up from 40% in 2024 and 23% in 2023. Someone on the floor hears that they are late. They are not late at wrapping lamps. They are being compared on a question about generative tools. Say the question or do not say the number.

The mirror mistake is shame from the smaller figure. 12.2% of businesses in the Statistics Canada count used AI to produce goods or deliver services in 2025, up from 6.1% a year earlier. An owner hears "almost nobody is really doing this" and either freezes or decides the whole topic is hype. Neither reaction picks a job. The smaller figure is a description of a strict question. It is not a personality test.

Averaging the two rates is the mistake we will interrupt out loud. A midpoint is not a readiness rate. The firms, the countries, and the verbs differ. This site does not publish an aggregate score from the Readiness Check, and we do not want a homemade one built in a spreadsheet either. If you need a number for your own business, it has to be about your business. A national blend cannot tell the bookkeeper whether the scan is readable.

Buying a tool to close the emotional gap is the costly version. The owner feels the distance between "we write blurbs" and "AI runs the shop," so she purchases something that promises to run the shop. Nothing in either survey asked her to do that. The surveys did not name her wholesaler, her chip in the glaze, or her bookkeeper's part-time days. A purchase that ignores those facts will stall for the ordinary reasons, and then the survey gets blamed.

Treating a yes on the Chamber question as a marketing claim is how trust frays. "We use AI" on the window, when what you use is a draft of a caption, invites a customer to expect something you do not do. We would rather the shop say nothing. The floor lead's honesty about the glaze is worth more than a badge.

What we'd do

We would plan from the job, and we would say the Statistics Canada question out loud before we said the Chamber one. That is a preference, not a law. Drafting help is legitimate. It is also the easier yes, and it flatters the owner. The question about producing goods or delivering services is the one that changes payroll, mistakes, and what the customer receives. If the owner only has appetite for the draft, we will help her keep it human-edited and small. We will not call it an operating shift.

We would not tell the home-goods owner she is behind the 58%, and we would not tell her she has "arrived" because she might be inside it. Arrival is a job you can name on a slow Tuesday, with a checker who is a person. The move from 23% in 2023 to 40% in 2024 to 58% in the Chamber's 2025 report shows generative use spreading. The move from 6.1% to 12.2% in the Statistics Canada figures for 2025 shows a stricter behavior spreading more quietly. Both can be true. Your shop does not have to impersonate either crowd.

If a partner brings you a chart this week, ask which question was on the survey. If they cannot answer, put the chart down and open the assessment. If they can answer, and the question is about use rather than delivery, read the help note before you spend. When you are ready to choose the job on purpose, the strategy note is the page we would write with you. We would rather be dull and specific than armed with a percentage that describes someone else's verb.

Questions

Why do the two surveys disagree?

They ask different questions. The Chamber counts generative AI use. Statistics Canada counts AI used to produce goods or deliver services.

What did the Chamber report?

In the 2025 report, 58% of small businesses said they use generative AI, up from 40% in 2024 and 23% in 2023.

What did Statistics Canada report?

12.2% of businesses used AI to produce goods or deliver services in 2025, up from 6.1% a year earlier.
