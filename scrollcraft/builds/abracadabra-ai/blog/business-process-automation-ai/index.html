Business Process Automation With AI: Write the Job

Write the steps before you add a model. Most knowledge workers already use AI. Few companies were planning training. Many proofs of concept still stop.

By Abracadabra Studio · 2026-10-14

Business process automation with AI starts as a written job. We mean the steps a person already follows, in the order they follow them, with the judgment marked where it actually sits. A model can’t follow a job that lives in someone’s head. It will invent the missing steps, and the invention will sound sure of itself. Anthropic’s December 2024 essay says to start with the simplest solution that works, and to prefer a fixed workflow until the next step actually depends on the last one.1 We treat that as operating advice. The tool comes after the page. If the page is blank, the tool is guessing.

If the steps aren’t written, stop spending. The process guide is the matching fix for that blank page. The automation note is which job to pick once you can see more than one. The agents note is the later question, when a fixed path honestly isn’t enough because the next step depends on what the last step found. Most rooms we sit in aren’t there yet. People are still telling the work from memory, and the telling changes every time we ask.

The process is the product

The map is the thing you are making. Software is only a way to run that map on a busy Tuesday the same way it ran on a quiet Monday. Microsoft’s 2024 index says 75% of knowledge workers use AI, and 46% of those users started less than six months earlier.2 Use is already ordinary. A shared plan is not. 60% of leaders worry leadership lacks a plan and vision. 55% of leaders were concerned about talent. Only 25% of companies were planning generative AI training. People are already in the tools. Almost nobody is teaching a shared way to use them.

Staff are already using AI, with or without a plan. 78% of AI users bring their own tools, and 80% do so at small and midsize businesses. At a small firm that usually means a personal login the owner likes and a different tab the office manager likes. The work lands in the shared inbox. The method stays in a browser history. When that person is out, the method is out. That isn’t a process. It’s a habit with a password. Banning the login without writing the path just moves the habit.

Where the hours sit should decide which job you write first. 60% of time in the work apps was spent in email, chat, and meetings, and 40% in creation apps. Owners often want to automate the part that feels like making something, and they leave the inbox alone. The inbox is the job. The chat thread is the job. The meeting that produces no note is the job. Inside those apps, creation is the smaller share of the time. Automate the daily sorting before you decorate a rare act of writing.

Gartner’s January 2026 article says at least 50% of generative AI projects were abandoned after proof of concept by the end of the prior year, citing poor data quality, inadequate risk controls, escalating costs, and unclear value.3 We don’t read that as a reason to fear the tools. We read it as a description of demos that never had a job attached. Poor data quality shows up when nobody decided which record is the real one. Inadequate risk controls show up when nobody owns the stop. Escalating costs show up when the proof keeps growing because the goal was never a single written path. Unclear value shows up when you can’t point at the step that got easier to finish. Then the project gets abandoned, and the writeup blames the model. The missing page never makes the writeup.

If the steps are not written, the model cannot follow them.

Run the Readiness Check

Write the steps a person already follows. Mark the step that waits for a judgment. Automate the steps that don’t. If every step needs a judgment, you don’t have a process yet. You have a craft. A craft can still be helped at the edges, with drafts and reminders and a place for the file to land. It shouldn’t be sold a machine that pretends to replace the judgment. We’d rather say that on the first call than watch someone fund a pilot that already matches the pile Gartner described.

A worked example

Take a trades desk as the example we walk through. This isn’t a measured result and it isn’t a trophy story. It’s the shape of the afternoon we keep seeing when we ask someone to narrate yesterday. The owner prices the work. The office manager answers the phone. A lead tech does the site visit and comes back with a note that is half measurements and half memory. The bookkeeper sends the invoice only after someone says the job is done.

Yesterday’s quote starts as a voicemail. The office manager scribbles the name, the street, the room, and a mention of a leak. The lead tech visits, takes photos on a personal phone, and texts a few of them to the owner with a line that says the job isn’t bad. The owner opens an old quote, changes a few lines, and sets the price from a similar job they remember. The bookkeeper never sees it until the customer says yes. Sometimes the yes arrives as a text to the owner, who means to forward it and doesn’t.

If we sit there and write the path, it becomes ordinary in the best way. The office manager logs the address and the room before the visit is booked. The lead tech files the measurements and the photos before leaving the driveway. The owner prices from that note, and the price is the step that waits. The office manager sends the quote only after the price is on it. The bookkeeper opens the invoice only after the yes is written down. We wouldn’t hand the price to a model. The rest is copying, ordering, and waiting for a mark. A simple system can carry the note, nag the tech for a missing measurement, and hold the quote. A person still sends it. What the bookkeeper wants is usually modest: a written yes, a matching price, and the same spelling of the name on the invoice. That’s a handoff.

A clinic has the same shape with different nouns. The front desk takes the call. The practitioner decides whether the visit is in scope. Billing waits. Write those steps and the front desk can draft the confirmation while a person still sends it. Skip the writing and someone buys a tool that claims to handle intake, while the practitioner keeps a side pad that becomes the real process. A shop counter looks simpler, which is why owners skip the page. The Saturday clerk and the owner disagree the first time we ask them both how a reorder actually happens. We write both versions down before anyone opens a settings screen.

How we’d run it

First, we ask the person who did the job yesterday to walk it without improving it. Not the ideal version. The version that happened, including the text and the sticky note. We write their words where they can see them. We don’t clean them up yet. Cleaning is how the wish sneaks in.

Second, we ask another person who touches the same job to walk it again. The office manager and the owner almost never tell the same story. Where they disagree, we don’t average them into a polite middle. We ask which version the customer actually received. That version wins, because the customer is the only audience that paid.

Third, we write the steps as short sentences a new hire could follow on a tired morning. Each sentence has a person and a thing they produce. The lead tech files the measurements before leaving the driveway. If a sentence has no output, it isn’t a step. It’s a mood, and moods don’t automate.

Fourth, we mark the step that waits for a judgment. On the trades desk that’s the price. At the clinic it might be whether the visit is in scope. At the shop it might be whether a regular gets a refund past the posted window. If we can’t find the mark, we aren’t ready to automate anything, because we don’t know what must stay with a person.

Fifth, we automate only the steps that don’t wait. Move the note. Remind the person. Hold the draft. File the photo. We use the simplest thing that does that move. A fixed workflow is enough when the next step doesn’t depend on a discovery inside the last one. The quote either has a price or it doesn’t. That’s a gate, not a reasoning problem. We stay with the gate even when a vendor calls it an agent.

Sixth, we run the written path on live jobs while the old path is still allowed, and we watch where people step around it. The workaround is the missing step. We add it to the page and we don’t scold the person who stepped around. They’re showing us the job we failed to write. We keep a person on the send. The draft can be prepared. The customer-facing word goes out because someone with a name looked at it. Skip that and you have a mailbox that talks.

Common mistakes

Buying the tool because a neighbor bought the tool is the mistake we see first. The neighbor’s job isn’t yours, and their judgment may sit somewhere else. We’ve sat with an owner who purchased a stack of seats, connected nothing to a real quote, and called the quiet that followed a failed pilot. The pilot never had a page. The invoice for the seats still arrived.

Writing the process you wish you had is the next one. The wished-for version has no voicemail and no promise to remember. The real version is made of those. Automate the wish and the staff will keep the real path in a group text while the new system looks empty. Empty gets blamed on adoption. The page described a company that doesn’t exist. Write the messy path. Remove one mess at a time after the messy path is visible.

Automating the judgment because it hurts has the best sales pitch. Pricing hurts. Deciding whether a visit is in scope hurts. Telling a customer no hurts. Pain isn’t a signal that a model should own the step. Move the chores around it and leave the painful step with a name. If the owner wants that step gone, we ask what they’ll do when the model is wrong in front of a customer. A shrug means we don’t build it.

Treating personal tools as a moral failure wastes time you don’t have. The share of people who bring their own tools is a description, and at small and midsize businesses that share is higher. Ban the personal login without a shared path and people will find another login. Give them the path, then show it on a real job. Training, which few companies were even planning, is how the path survives the first busy stretch.

Calling a proof of concept a strategy is how budgets disappear. A proof of concept is a test of a written step. With no step, you have a demo. When the value stays unclear, the project joins the abandoned pile. Skipping the person who lives in the path is the same mistake in a quieter voice. If the office manager wasn’t in the room, the map won’t match the phone. If they stumble while reading a sentence aloud, the sentence is wrong.

What we’d do

We’d refuse the model until the steps fit on one page and a person who does the job has nodded at each line. We hold that opinion when the owner is eager and a vendor is already in the hallway. Eagerness isn’t a process. A hallway demo isn’t a process. A pile of licenses isn’t a process. The page is the process. Everything else is a way to run it.

We’d pick the job that already happens every day in email, chat, or the meeting note, because that’s where the hours already are. We’d mark the judgment and keep a named person on it. We’d let a fixed workflow carry the rest. We wouldn’t start with an agent or a custom build. If the next step truly depends on what the last step found, we can talk about something smarter, after someone shows us that branch on a real job from last week.

We’d also say the quiet part about training. People are using these tools. Few companies were planning to teach a shared way. A path nobody is shown on a real quote is a folder nobody opens. If you want a test before you spend anything, ask yesterday’s doer to write the steps from memory, then ask the person who receives the output what they wish they’d received. If those pages don’t match, you’ve found the work. Name a product after that, not before.

Questions

Should you automate before you write the steps?

No. Anthropic says to start with the simplest system that works. A model on an unwritten job invents the steps.

What share of companies were planning AI training?

Microsoft’s 2024 index says only 25% of companies were planning generative AI training, while 75% of knowledge workers already use AI.

What happens to proofs of concept?

Gartner’s 2026 article says at least 50% of generative AI projects were abandoned after proof of concept.
