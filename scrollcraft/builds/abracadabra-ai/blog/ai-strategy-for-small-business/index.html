AI Strategy for Small Business: Start With the Job

Name the job, the data, and who checks the output before you buy a tool. Use is already common. A written plan and staff training are not.

By Abracadabra Studio · 2026-10-14

An AI strategy for a small business is a short list you can say out loud: the job that changes, the data it needs, and the person who checks the output. It is not a tool catalog, and it is not a slogan about the future. If the page cannot name those three things, you do not have a strategy. You have a pile of apps and a hope that the pile will become a plan by being large.

The assessment scores the same groups of facts we want on that page. The implementation framework is the sequence after the job is named, when you are ready to do the work in order. The readiness framework is the scoring sheet underneath both. Start with the job. The tools are allowed to wait in the other room, unopened, until the page exists.

We have watched owners skip the page because it felt too small to be serious. Serious, in a company of this size, is one job that survives a busy week. A binder that names every department you do not have is theater.

Use is ahead of the plan

Microsoft’s 2024 Work Trend Index says 75% of knowledge workers use AI at work.1 46% of those users started less than six months before the survey. 60% of leaders worry their organization’s leadership lacks a plan and vision to implement AI. 78% of AI users bring their own tools, and that share is 80% at small and midsize businesses. 55% of leaders are concerned about talent. Only 25% of companies were planning to offer generative AI training. Read that as a picture of a truck cab and a front office that have not met. People are already using the tools. The plan, the training, and the talent worry are behind them.

NIST’s risk framework groups the work as Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage.2 A strategy that cannot point at those jobs has not started. Govern is who is allowed to decide. Map is where the work and the data actually are. Measure is how you will know the output is wrong. Manage is what you do on the week it is wrong. If your page is only a list of product names, you have skipped all four.

Salesforce surveyed 3,350 leaders at firms of 200 employees and under.3 The fieldwork ran through August and September 2024. 75% said they were at least experimenting with AI. Among growing small and midsize businesses, 83% said that. 78% of growing firms planned to increase AI investment, against 55% of firms that were declining. 91% of those already using AI said it boosts revenue. Those are leaders describing their own companies. They are not a promise we will repeat to you as if your revenue had already answered.

Those figures describe different populations. Microsoft asked knowledge workers and leaders. Salesforce asked small-business leaders. Neither figure is a substitute for the job you can name on one page. A worker who brings a tool from home, and a leader who says revenue moved, can both be telling the truth about different questions. Your page still has to pick a job.

A worked example

Walk a heating-and-cooling company that runs a handful of trucks. Example only. Nobody measured this for a report. The owner prices jobs from the driver’s seat and has started pasting symptom notes into a personal chatbot. He likes the draft it gives him. He has not told the dispatcher. The dispatcher still builds the board from calls, texts, and a whiteboard that is more accurate than the software they pay for. Two techs trust the board. They do not trust a paragraph the owner forwards at night.

There is no strategy yet, just staff quietly using their own tools. That habit is exactly the pattern in the Microsoft index, where most AI users bring their own tools, and the share is even higher at small and midsize businesses. The owner is not a villain. He found something that unsticks a quote when he is tired. The trouble starts when a customer reads the draft back to the dispatcher the next morning, including a part the shop does not stock and a price the owner would have softened if he had been at a desk.

The office manager finds out in the worst way, which is from the customer. She has no page that says which jobs the tool may touch. She has no rule that a quote leaves only after she or the dispatcher has seen it. She was never trained, because there was nothing to be trained on except “the boss has an app.” The Microsoft index saying only a minority of companies were planning generative AI training will feel familiar to her. She is living in the gap between use and a plan.

Here is the page we would write with them, still without a tool name on it. The job is draft estimates for replacement jobs, not for complaints and not for anything that sounds like a safety issue. The data is the price book on the office server, plus the notes the tech already puts on the ticket, and not the owner’s memory of a similar house. The checker is the dispatcher, who already knows which crews can do the work this week. If the dispatcher is out, the office manager is the backup, named. If the draft mentions a part they do not stock, it does not leave. That is a strategy. It is short enough to tape inside the cabinet.

Notice what we did not put on the page. We did not put “explore AI across the business.” We did not put a chatbot on the website. We did not promise the techs a new identity. The techs need the board to stay right. The owner’s night-time draft can survive only if it becomes a draft the dispatcher expects, from sources the office already trusts. Until then, the honest instruction is: stop forwarding the chat to customers.

How we’d run it

We build the page before we add a login. The order matters more than the stationery.

First, we ask the owner what he already does in private. The chatbot in the truck is the fact. We do not scold it. We write it down as bring-your-own use, which is common, and we separate it from a company plan, which is not. Hiding the habit makes it dangerous. Naming it makes it editable.

Second, we pick one job and write the fence around it. Replacement estimates in. Complaint threads out. Safety calls out. Anything the price book cannot support out. The fence is the strategy. A job without a fence becomes every job by Friday, because the tool will answer any question you type. Willingness is not permission.

Third, we name the data and the checker in the same sitting. For this company the price book and the ticket notes are in, and the owner’s unwritten memory is out. The dispatcher checks. The office manager covers. We put a hold on the dispatcher’s calendar so “check” is a time and not a virtue. NIST’s Map and Measure live here, even if we never say the words in the room. Govern is the owner agreeing the fence is real. Manage is what they do when a draft fails the fence: it does not get forwarded, and the reason gets said out loud once so the techs hear the rule.

Fourth, we train only the people on that page. The dispatcher and the office manager, on this job, with real tickets from last week. We do not schedule an all-hands about the future of the trades. Only 25% of companies in the Microsoft index were planning generative AI training at all. We would rather be inside that planning group for one role than outside it with a motivational meeting. The techs get a simpler lesson: quotes to customers come from the office, not from a forwarded chat.

Fifth, we leave the revenue claim alone. Salesforce heard leaders say use boosts revenue, including a very large share of those already using AI. We will not print that on this company’s page as if it were their result. Their result, if they earn one, will be a dispatcher who can clear draft estimates without a surprise part. If they want a score before they start, the assessment and the readiness framework are the sheets. If they want the order of work after the page exists, that is the implementation framework.

Common mistakes

Writing the strategy as a shopping list is the mistake the vendors hope for. A list of products is not a job, a data source, or a checker. It is a set of invoices. Microsoft’s leaders were already worried, at 60%, that leadership lacked a plan and vision to implement AI. A shopping list will not calm that worry. It will add logins.

Pretending bring-your-own use is not happening is the fragile mistake. 78% of AI users bring their own tools, and that share is 80% at small and midsize businesses. Your owner in the truck is normal. If the plan ignores him, he will keep forwarding drafts, and the plan will describe a company that does not exist. Fold the habit in, or forbid it in a sentence he agrees to. Do not do neither.

Training “the staff” in the abstract is how training stays imaginary. The people who need it are the dispatcher and the office manager. A general session lets everyone nod and changes no ticket. The index is blunt that planning for generative AI training was uncommon. Copying that absence is easy. A short session on one job is the repair.

Borrowing another population’s revenue line is the flattering mistake. 91% of Salesforce respondents already using AI said it boosts revenue. They were leaders at firms of 200 employees and under, in fieldwork that ran through August and September 2024. They are allowed to say that about themselves. You are not allowed to treat it as your forecast. 75% at least experimenting, and 83% among growing small and midsize businesses, is still a description of leaders, not of your whiteboard.

Skipping Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage because the names sound like a big institution is the last miss. The names are just drawers. Who decides. Where the facts are. How you notice a bad output. What you do next. A heating company can fill those drawers on a single page. If it will not, the strategy has not started, no matter how current the owner’s app feels.

What we’d do

We would tape one page inside the cabinet and we would refuse to start a tool rollout until the page had names on it. That is the opinion we bring into the room. Use is already ahead of the plan. 75% of knowledge workers in the Microsoft index use AI at work, and 46% of those users had started less than six months before they were asked. The plan is the scarce object. Chasing more use, when use is the thing you already have in the truck, is how you stay in the 60% of leaders who worry there is no vision. Make the vision a fence around one job.

We would not tell this owner that AI boosts revenue because 91% of leaders who were already using it said so to Salesforce. We believe leaders answer surveys about their own story. We do not believe that story transfers to a dispatcher who has not seen the draft. 78% of growing firms planned to increase investment, against 55% of firms that were declining. Investment without a checker is how declining firms get a new bill. Spend on the price book, the dispatcher’s time, and a rule the techs can repeat.

We would also be plain about talent. 55% of leaders in the Microsoft index are concerned about it, and we share a narrower version of that concern. You probably do not need a new kind of employee. You need the dispatcher to be allowed to reject the owner’s draft. That is a talent decision disguised as a software decision. If you cannot give her that permission, do not buy anything. Go back to the readiness framework until the permission exists. Then the implementation framework can run, and the assessment will have something real to score. A strategy that the dispatcher can spoil is a strategy. A strategy only the owner understands is a hobby with a heading.

Questions

What belongs in a small-business AI strategy?

The job that changes, the data it needs, who checks the output, and how you will know it failed. A tool name comes after those answers.

What share of knowledge workers already use AI?

Microsoft’s 2024 index says 75% of knowledge workers use AI at work, and 78% of AI users bring their own tools.

Does high use mean the staff were trained?

No. The same Microsoft index says only 25% of companies were planning generative AI training.
