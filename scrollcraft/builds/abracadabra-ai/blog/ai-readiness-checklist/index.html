AI Readiness Checklist Before You Buy a Tool

Questions to answer before you buy an AI tool, grouped by data, process, people, and the decision. Public surveys name the gaps those questions catch.

By Christopher Buzny · 2026-10-14

An AI readiness checklist is the list you finish before you pay for a tool. We use it when an owner has a demo booked and has not yet named the job. The questions fall into four groups: data, process, people, and the decision. A vendor's feature list is not a substitute for those questions.

The Readiness Check scores the same four. The assessment note compares the public tools. The data note stays on the first group. If the process score is the weak one, the process guide is the page we hand over.

We think of the checklist as a brake. The tour looks tidy. The real test is the busy afternoon after it, when nobody remembers which screen is the real one. A tick means you can point. A blank means you cannot, and the blank is the useful part.

Data and process

Can you name the source of truth for the job? Is it current, and can a system read it without someone retyping it? Is the job written down, including the exceptions, and does one person own the handoff?

We ask them out loud, in front of the person who does the work, not only the person who signs the check. The owner answers from memory. The person at the counter answers from last Thursday. When they disagree, the list has already earned its keep.

Every shop we sit in has more than one place a fact can live: the notebook, the spreadsheet the bookkeeper trusts, the supplier portal, the text thread. The record you mean is whichever of those the next person will believe when they conflict. If you cannot name that one, a new tool becomes another place to hunt.

Then ask whether it is current, and whether a system can read it. A source patched by memory is a souvenir. For an open order, current means the status changed when the part arrived, not when someone remembered to call. If someone still has to retype the record, the tool will wait on that person. Retyping is the job you have not replaced.

The process questions sit beside the data questions because a clean record with no written handoff still stalls. We want the happy path and the exceptions. What happens when the part is wrong, when the customer does not answer, or when the usual person is out? If those live as habit, the tool will follow the happy path and invent the rest. One person has to own the handoff on the day that person is not there. "We all own it" is how a handoff disappears.

Gartner reported in February 2025 that 63% of respondents do not have, or are unsure they have, the data management practices AI needs.1 The same note says that through 2026 organizations will abandon 60% of AI projects that lack AI-ready data. We read that as permission to treat the data questions as the main event. A large firm and a counter can fail the same way. The record is not ready, and the project dies after the demo fades.

AWS published a checklist in September 2025.2 It walks five steps and sorts a business into four stages: Not yet ready, Emerging, Operational, and Scaling. The page does not cite an adoption percentage. We like that restraint. A stage name is a description you can argue with. If a page will not tell you how many firms it counted, do not borrow its silence and turn it into a rate.

We use those stage names as labels, not as a grade. Not yet ready means the job is still in one person's head. Emerging means some of it is written and a lot of it is habit. Operational means a team can run the job without a hero. Scaling means you are repeating a pattern, not discovering it. You do not have to adopt the labels. You do have to say which one fits.

The process guide we linked above is what we open when this half of the list is mostly blank. Fix the writing before you fix the software. A short written handoff, with the exceptions on the same page, beats a tool that assumes the handoff already exists.

People and the decision

Who may use a tool, and who may send its output to a customer? Those are the people questions, and they are where checklists go soft. "The team will use it" is not an answer. A name is an answer. A rule about what can leave the building is an answer.

Microsoft's 2024 Work Trend Index says 75% of knowledge workers use AI at work, and 46% of those users started less than six months earlier.3 78% of AI users bring their own tools. At small and midsize companies that share is 80%. 60% of leaders worry their organization's leadership lacks a plan and vision to implement AI.

In a small company those figures show up as a person. Someone is already pasting notes into a personal account because the official way is slow. They are trying to finish the day. A checklist that never asks who brought a tool in will miss the real system, which is often the one in a coat pocket.

We ask who touches the tool on a normal day, who may send its words to a customer, and who can stop it when it is wrong. If nobody can stop it, you do not have a people plan. You have a hope that errors will be obvious.

The decision questions are separate. What would count as success, who can stop the experiment, and what happens if the tool is wrong? "Sam will keep an eye on it" is a person. "The customer hears from the record, not from memory" is a decision. "If it sends a wrong message, we turn it off the same day" is a decision. Write them on different lines.

A tick list is not a decision. Run the Readiness Check.

Run the Readiness Check

A tick list does not say which gap is largest. The Readiness Check turns the same four groups into a band. We still want the paper list first, because a band without a conversation is a costume on a number. The list is the conversation. The band is what you get when you want the same questions scored the same way next time.

One more decision question we add when the room is being polite. What will you stop doing if this works? If the answer is nothing, the tool is a hobby. The checklist is allowed to end with a no. A no that names the blank box is a better outcome than a yes that names a logo.

A worked example

Say we are sitting after close with the owner of a neighborhood hardware shop. We will call the owner June, the counter lead Chris, and the bookkeeper Anita. This is an example we walk through. It is not a report of a shop we measured.

June wants a tool that drafts the message saying a special order is in, and that suggests which supplier to chase. The demo was slick. The checklist is the part where we ignore the demo.

We start with data. June says the source of truth is the computer. Chris keeps a notebook because the computer is wrong when a quantity changes at the counter. Anita trusts a spreadsheet she updates from supplier emails, because the other two disagree and she has to pay someone. The box for one source of truth stays blank. Asked which record he would trust if June were out, Chris says the notebook, then says he would call Anita. The source of truth is a person. A person can be the bridge. A person cannot be the record a tool reads.

Then the process. A part arrives, someone calls, the customer picks up, Anita marks it paid. That happy path is easy. A damaged part goes on the back shelf until Chris "deals with it." A customer who does not answer gets one more try, then the order sits. None of that is written, and on days Chris is at the other site the handoff has no owner. We refuse to count the happy path as the whole process.

Then people. Chris already uses a chat tool on his phone to rewrite messages when he is tired of sounding stiff. June did not know. We do not scold him. We write the question the checklist was for. Who may send those words to a customer, and who may not?

Then the decision. June says success is less chaos. We push once. Success is that a customer hears about an arrival from the record, not from Chris's memory, and that Anita can see the same status Chris sees. If a message goes out wrong, June can stop the trial herself. She does not wait for a vendor. That line is what lets her buy later without betting the shop on a login.

We leave with blanks on data and process, a surprise on people, and a decision that is finally a sentence. Nobody bought anything. That is the checklist working.

How we'd run it

We run it as a short working session, not as homework we email into the void.

First we pick one job. Not the whole business. One job with a start and a finish. In the shop, that job is the special order from the promise to the moment the customer has the part or has been told it failed. If you pick every job, you will tick nothing honestly.

Second we put the people who touch that job in the room. The owner alone will describe the job she wishes existed. The counter lead will describe the job that exists. The bookkeeper will describe the job that gets paid. All three answers belong on the sheet.

Third we walk data, then process, then people, then the decision, and we write blanks in ink. We do not shop for tools in the middle. If someone says the new software would handle that, we write "not handled today" and keep going.

Fourth we read the blanks back and name one owner for the next change. If the source of truth is the blank that matters, Anita and Chris agree what they will treat as the record on ordinary days. June's job is to back that choice when it is inconvenient.

Fifth we only then look at tools, with the sheet on the table. Any feature that does not touch a ticked box or a named blank is a distraction. If the blanks are still the whole story, we stop. A process blank goes to the process guide. The same four groups go to the check when we want a band instead of ticks.

We book the session after the rush, short enough that Chris is not watching the door. A checklist filled by the owner alone at midnight will be full, and it will be wrong.

Common mistakes

The mistake we see most is ticking the box you hope is true. "We have a process" often means a binder from a quieter year. If Chris cannot find the page that covers a damaged part, you do not have a process for the job you are buying for.

Another mistake is letting the vendor's questionnaire replace yours. Their questions ask whether you can be a customer. Yours ask whether the job can survive contact with a tool. Keep yours, even if yours is a notebook page.

A third mistake is calling a shared inbox a system of record. An inbox is a pile that happens to be searchable. It is current only if someone triaged it. The checklist asks whether a system can read the record without a person retyping. A pile fails that test.

We also watch owners score their own confidence and write only the happy path. Confidence is a mood. The checklist is a pointing exercise. The exceptions are the list.

What we'd do

We would not buy in the meeting where the checklist is first filled in. We would buy later, for one job, after the source of truth has a name and the handoff has a person. Our opinion is blunt. A tool on top of three records will make another record, and you will pay a fee for the privilege of still calling Anita.

If we were June, we would spend the next stretch of ordinary days making Chris and Anita write status in one place, including the damaged-part case, and we would pause Chris's phone tool for customer messages until June has said what may be sent. That is a smaller project than a new platform. It is the project the checklist was trying to start.

We would treat a full page of ticks with suspicion, and a short page of blanks with respect. The blanks are the work. The tool can wait.

Questions

What should an AI readiness checklist cover?

Data, the written process, the people who will use the output, and the decision about what success means. A tool purchase is the last step, not the first.

Does the AWS checklist publish an adoption rate?

No. The September 2025 page walks five steps and names four stages. It does not cite a percentage of businesses that are ready.

Why ask who brings their own AI tool?

Microsoft's 2024 index says 78% of AI users bring their own tools, and 80% at small and midsize businesses. A checklist that ignores that will miss the tools already in the building.
