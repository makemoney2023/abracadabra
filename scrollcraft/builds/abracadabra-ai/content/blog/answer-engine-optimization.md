Answer engine optimization is the work of being cited when a generative answer is written, and Google still treats that work as ordinary SEO. The new name does not create a new set of rules for your site.

Google's guide says that work is still SEO.[^google] The page has to be indexed, and it has to be eligible for a snippet. Google says it does not use llms.txt, and it says there is no special chunking requirement. No extra schema is required for those features. We translate that for owners as a short list of refusals. You do not get a special file that makes you the answer. You do not get to hide the real page behind a blob of text a person cannot read. You do the unglamorous thing. You publish a page Google can index, a page a snippet can lift, and a page a customer can finish.

The [visibility guide](https://check.abra-ca-dabra.app/check/guide/visibility) is the matching check for whether the site can be cited at all. The [ChatGPT note](/blog/how-to-get-your-business-to-show-up-in-chatgpt) covers crawler files, which are a different switch from this one. The [GEO comparison](/blog/generative-engine-optimization-vs-seo) is the research split, if you want the paper and the click study side by side.

## What the research measured

A 2024 paper on generative engine optimization reports visibility gains of up to 40%.[^geo] Methods that add citations, quotations, and statistics were associated with gains over 40%. On Perplexity the reported gain reached 37%. Position-adjusted word count rose 41%, and a subjective impression score rose 28%. The benchmark used 10,000 queries. Those are research results on pages written to be easier for a generated answer to lift. They are not a promise that your faucet page will move by a set amount because you added a quote.

What we take from the paper, sitting with an owner, is the kind of sentence that got the lift. A citation points at a source. A quotation is someone specific saying a thing you can check. A statistic is a number with a population attached, the way this paragraph is doing. A page that says "we are the best hardware store in town" has none of those. A page that says how a cartridge is measured, quotes the manufacturer's fit note, and tells the reader which handles in the aisle match it, has something an answer can carry. The gain is attached to that kind of writing. It is not attached to the phrase "answer engine" in a footer.

Pew, in July 2025, found that visits with an AI summary produced a click 8% of the time, against 15% when no summary appeared.[^pew] A citation that nobody clicks is still a citation. Write the page so the click is worth taking.

The drop is the part owners feel in the till, even when they never see the study. If the summary already told the person the cartridge size, they may not need you for the fact. They still need you for the part in stock, the person who will show them the wrench, and the honest line about what you will not do on a Sunday. Put that on the page. The summary can borrow the measurement. The visit is for the counter.

## A worked example

Walk a neighborhood hardware store. This is an example we talk through, not a result. Ruth owns it. Sam works the counter and knows which aisle lies. The question people already ask, out loud and in search, is some version of "can someone cut a key and tell me which cartridge fits this dripping faucet."

The old page says the store sells hardware, paint, and keys, with a phone number and a cheerful sentence about friendly service. It is indexed, barely, and a snippet would have nothing sharp to lift. An answer engine, asked that faucet question, will cite a page that states the measurement, the common mismatch, and what to bring to the counter. Ruth's page can be that page. It does not become that page by adding a new acronym to the title.

Sam's knowledge is the raw material. He already says, to the person holding a bag of parts, that the stem length matters more than the brand stamped on the handle, and that a photo of the old cartridge saves a second trip. That sentence belongs at the top, in words a summary can quote and a person can use. Under it, Ruth writes what the store actually does. Keys cut while you wait on the days Sam is in. Cartridges matched if you bring the old one or a clear photo. No plumbing repairs in the wall. The refusal is useful. It keeps the answer honest, and it keeps the click from becoming a disappointed visit.

Roles, not a scoreboard. Ruth owns the claims and will not let a page say they repair what they only sell. Sam checks the draft against what he says at the counter, because a polished sentence that contradicts him will make the next customer angry. Whoever updates the site, even if that is a nephew, has one job: the page stays indexable, the first sentences answer the question, and the source of any number is visible. If a figure is about their own stock, they say it is theirs. If it comes from a manufacturer sheet, they name the sheet. That is the citations-and-quotations move, done at the size of one shop.

A generated answer might then say the stem length matters and send the person to Ruth. Pew's finding is why the page cannot stop at the fact the summary will steal. The summary can explain the stem. The page has to make the trip obviously better. Bring the old cartridge. Sam is the one who matches it. The store does not open the wall. Those lines are the reason to click, and they are also the reason the citation does not become a complaint.

## How we'd run it

We would not open with a tool that "does AEO." We would open the page a customer already lands on and ask whether a stranger could act on it.

First, confirm the page is indexed and eligible for a snippet. If it is blocked, thin, or tucked behind a form, stop. Google's guide is blunt that generative features still need an ordinary page. No file substitutes for that.

Second, write the question in the customer's words at the top, then answer it in the next sentences. Key cutting, cartridge match, what to bring. A heading that restates the question helps a person scan, and it gives an answer something clean to lift. This is structure, not a special chunking ritual. Google says there is no special chunking requirement.

Third, add the material the 2024 paper associated with the gains. A real citation, a real quotation, a real figure. For Ruth, that might be the manufacturer fit note, a sentence that is actually Sam's, and a plain statement of which sizes they stock. Do not invent a study. Do not borrow a number you cannot stand behind if a customer reads it back to you.

Fourth, add the reason to click, knowing a summary may already have stated the fact. Hours in words, what you refuse, who at the counter does the matching, what the person should carry in. Pew's July 2025 read is the context. A click happened 8% of the time when a summary appeared, against 15% when it did not. Write for the person who still has a reason to come.

Fifth, skip the extras Google says it does not need. Do not wait on llms.txt. Do not add schema you cannot explain. Do not rewrite the page as fragments that only a model could love. A person in the aisle is the reader who can hurt you.

Sixth, read the page out loud at the counter. Sam corrects anything he would not say. Ruth deletes any boast. Then leave it up long enough to see whether the questions at the register start to match the questions on the page. That is the feedback that matters at this size. A visibility score from a vendor is optional. The counter is not.

## Common mistakes

The first mistake is treating the new name as a new channel with its own file. Google says this is still SEO, and it says it does not use llms.txt. A shop that spends the week on a special file and leaves the faucet page vague has done the opposite of the guide.

The second mistake is a page that cannot be lifted. No clear answer in the opening lines, no heading a person can scan, a phone number as the only fact. Eligibility for a snippet is a requirement, not a style preference. If a snippet would be empty, an answer will be empty too.

The third mistake is fake precision. The paper's gains, up to 40%, and over 40% where citations, quotations, and statistics were added, describe methods on a benchmark of 10,000 queries. Pasting those percentages into Ruth's title would be a lie about her store. Use the method. Do not wear the result.

The fourth mistake is writing only for the summary. You optimize the fact, and you forget the click. When a summary is present, Pew saw a click 8% of the time, against 15% without one. A perfect citation that sends nobody to the counter is a thinner business, even if a dashboard calls it visibility.

The fifth mistake is contradictions between the page and the counter. The page says same-day key cutting. Sam is off, and the backup will not touch the machine. The answer cites you, the customer arrives, and you lose the only asset that mattered, which is being believed next time.

The sixth mistake is extra schema as a superstition. The guide says no extra schema is required for these features. Adding markup you do not understand does not repair a page that never answered the question.

## What we'd do

We would rewrite the one page people already need, in Sam's words, with the refusal included, and we would ignore the pitch that AEO is a separate craft with a separate invoice. Our opinion is that the paper is useful and the label is not. Visibility gains in a 2024 study are a reason to put citations, quotations, and statistics on the page when you truly have them. They are not a reason to talk differently from the way you talk at the counter.

Google still wants an indexed page a snippet can use. Pew says the click gets rarer when a summary shows up. So the page has to be worth the trip after the answer has already spoken. We would rather Ruth be cited for a true sentence about stem length, and then earn the visit, than chase a file Google has said it does not read. If you do one thing after this, open the page that should win the question and see whether a stranger could follow it. That is the work. The acronym can wait in the hallway.
