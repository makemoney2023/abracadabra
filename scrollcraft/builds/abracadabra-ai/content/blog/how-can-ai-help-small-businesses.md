AI helps a small business by taking a named job a person already does. It does not help by moving you closer to a headline. Two surveys from 2025 count AI in small firms, and they count different work. The U.S. Chamber asked who uses generative AI. Statistics Canada asked who used AI to produce goods or deliver services. A firm can say yes to the first and no to the second, and both answers can be honest. This note keeps those claims apart, then names the jobs owners hand over. The [pressure guide](https://check.abra-ca-dabra.app/check/guide/pressure) is the matching question for one business. [Christopher Buzny](/about/christopher-buzny) writes these notes from the [studio site](/).

We sit with owners who have already been told they are late. The number they heard was usually the larger one. Our job in that first conversation is to slow the number down until it has a population attached. Help, for a shop or a clinic or a trades desk, is a draft someone checks, a sort someone trusts, a reminder someone still sends. Help is not a rank on a national chart.

## Generative use is not production use

The Chamber's Empowering Small Business report says 58% of small businesses self-identified that they use generative AI, up from 40% in 2024 and 23% in 2023.[^chamber] That question asks about generative AI. It does not ask whether AI produces the goods or the service. Self-identified means the owner said so. It doesn't mean an auditor watched the work. It doesn't mean the tool is in the product. An owner who drafts a customer email and then rewrites half of it can answer yes. We would answer yes with them. That yes is real. It is also a different fact from a system that makes the thing the customer bought.

Statistics Canada, in the Canadian Survey on Business Conditions for the second quarter of 2025, found that 12.2% of businesses used AI to produce goods or deliver services, up from 6.1% in the second quarter of 2024.[^statcan] The release is dated 16 June 2025. Collection ran from 1 April to 5 May 2025. The agency received 21,357 responses, of which 9,103 came from businesses that used AI. Read the response counts as a picture of who was asked, not as a target. A large response still describes the questions that were asked. It doesn't turn the generative question into the production question.

<!--image:2-->

Among businesses that used AI, text analytics accounted for 35.7%, and marketing automation for 23.1%. 40.1% had developed new workflows, and 38.9% had trained staff. Those shares sit inside the group that already uses AI. They are not shares of every firm in the country, and they are not shares of the Chamber's generative question. Text analytics is reading and sorting language. Marketing automation is sending and measuring. A new workflow is a path that wasn't written before. Training is a person who was shown the path. Put that way, the list is practical. It is also modest. None of those four is a claim that the company now runs itself.

## Jobs an owner can hand over

The Chamber figure describes a tool someone opens. The StatCan figure describes AI inside the delivery of the product or the service. A small business can do the first for a long time without the second. We think that is a sane order. Open the tool on a job you can check. Let production use be a later question, asked only when the goods or the service actually depend on the system. Most owners we sit with are nowhere near that later question, and they don't need to pretend they are.

The jobs that show up in the StatCan breakdown are the practical list: reading and sorting text, sending and measuring marketing, writing a workflow that did not exist, and training the people who will run it. None of those is a promise that the tool will run the company. Reading text looks like an inbox, a pile of referrals, a supplier sheet. Sending marketing looks like a note to people who already bought, not a brand campaign with a film crew. A new workflow looks like a page the front desk can follow when the owner is in a room with a patient. Training looks like sitting with that person once on a real message, not a slide deck titled for a company with a department you don't have.

The [readiness note](/blog/ai-readiness-assessment) asks a different question: whether this firm, not the average firm, has the data, the process, the people, and the decision in place. Average firms are what surveys are for. Your firm is a counter, a desk, a schedule. If the readiness questions fail, the survey percentages will not save the project. If they pass, you still don't need the larger percentage as a goal. You need the job.

## A worked example

Take a clinic as the walkthrough. The owner saw the Chamber figure and felt late, because friends in a group chat were "already using it." The front desk still types appointment reminders by hand. The practitioner still scribbles the after-visit note. Billing still waits for someone to say the visit happened. Nothing in that room is producing care through a model. The care is the practitioner. The model, if it enters at all, enters as a draft of a reminder or a cleaner version of a note the practitioner will still sign.

Walk the reminder, because it's the job that happens every day and a wrong one is obvious. The front desk has the visit on the book. A draft can say the time, the place, and what to bring. The front desk reads it, because some patients need a phone call and some need a short message, and the draft doesn't know the difference. The practitioner isn't in that decision unless the reminder includes a clinical instruction. If it does, the practitioner reads it. Billing doesn't need to see it. That's three roles and one job. We named the handoff.

A shop down the street is even plainer, which is why the headline feels insulting there. The owner sorts supplier mail and customer questions in the same inbox. Sorting is text work. A system can group the supplier sheets away from the sizing questions. The owner still decides which question gets answered before lunch. The goods on the shelf were not produced by a model. Saying the shop "uses AI" because the inbox got grouped is the Chamber kind of yes. Saying the shop uses AI to produce the goods would be the StatCan question, and the honest answer is no.

A trades desk sits between them. The lead tech's site note is text, and turning it into a description the customer can read is a draft. The owner still sets the price. The service is still the repair. You are near the production question only when delivery of the job actually depends on the system, not because a friend forwarded a chart. Until then, hand over the draft and keep the price.

## How we'd run it

First, write down which survey sentence you heard. If it was about generative use, say so in a full sentence. If it was about producing the goods or the service, say that instead. Owners mix them into "most businesses are already doing this," which is not what either source said. The populations are different, and the Canadian production share is the quieter fact.

Second, name one job on your floor that matches the practical list. Reading and sorting. A message you send more than once. A path that isn't written. A person who hasn't been shown the path. Pick the one that already hurts this week. Don't pick the one that would look impressive in a post.

Third, write the roles the way the clinic reminder was written. Who drafts. Who must read. Who is not involved. If everyone is involved, you don't have a handoff. You have a meeting. Training is showing the person who will actually run it.

Fourth, keep the output in a waiting place until the named person acts. A reminder sits. A sorted inbox sits. A rewritten note sits. The send, the order, the published page: those stay human until you can say what happens when they're wrong. For a clinic, wrong means a patient showed up on the wrong day. For a shop, wrong means a regular got a chilly note. For a trades desk, wrong means a price the owner never set. If you can't say the wrongness, you aren't ready to let the draft travel.

Fifth, only after that job has been dull for a stretch, ask the production question on purpose. Does the good or the service depend on this system, or does a person still make the thing while the system handles words around it? If a person still makes the thing, you are in the generative pattern, and that is a legitimate place to stay. The smaller production share is not a reprimand. It's a count of a harder change.

Sixth, check readiness for this firm, not for the average firm. Data you trust, a process someone can say aloud, a person who will own the check, and a decision about what not to automate. The readiness note is that conversation. The pressure guide asks where the business actually hurts. Neither one is a national percentage.

## Common mistakes

The mistake we correct most often is averaging the two surveys into one mood. The Chamber series shows a rise in owners who say they use generative AI. The Canadian series shows a much smaller share using AI to produce goods or deliver services, and it shows what AI-using firms changed afterward. Those are neighbors, not twins. Quote one and you've told a different story than the speaker told. We've heard pitches that borrow the larger figure and then promise the smaller figure's outcome. That's how a draft tool gets sold as a new way to deliver the service.

The next mistake is treating a self-identified yes as a finished operation.  The owner who tried a chatbot on a Sunday can say they use it. The front desk may never have seen the login. The survey can't see that gap, because it asked the business, not each role. When we sit down we ask the person who does the job, not the person who read the article. If those two answers differ, believe the doer.

Reading the tool shares as a quota is how small firms buy things they won't run. Text analytics and marketing automation are common among the Canadian businesses that already use AI. Common is not mandatory. A clinic with no marketing program doesn't need a marketing project so a share looks familiar. A shop that never publishes a page doesn't need a content stack. The workflow share and the training share interest us more than the tool shares, because they describe work after the tool arrived. A firm that adopted, didn't change the path, and didn't show anyone is the firm we worry about, even if their yes was confident.

Skipping the population is how "9,103" starts to sound like a club you failed to join. That figure is responses from businesses that used AI, inside a larger set of responses, in a survey with a collection window. It isn't the number of businesses that have figured out your counter. Use it to remember the claim is grounded. Don't use it as a mirror.

The last mistake is letting the headline pick the job. The owner wants whatever the article seemed to praise. The floor wants the reminder or the inbox. We follow the floor. The article already gave us clean questions. It doesn't get to staff the front desk.

## What we'd do

We'd tell the owner the larger figure and the smaller figure in the same sitting, with the question attached to each, and we'd refuse to pick a tool until they can say which question they actually care about. Most of the time they care about a job, not about production. That's our opinion, and we think it's the kinder one. Chasing the production share before the reminder is reliable is how you buy a system the practitioner will route around.

We'd hand over reading and sorting first, because the inbox is already there. We'd keep a person on anything a customer could feel. We'd write the workflow only for the job we just named, and we'd train the person who runs it before we trained anyone else. A slide for the whole staff can wait. The front desk cannot.

We wouldn't use either survey as a score for the firm in front of us. Surveys measure everyone. The readiness questions measure you. Write the job the way the customer feels it, name the person who checks it, and ignore every percentage until that sentence exists. The percentages will still be true in the morning. Your counter is the thing that can slip.
