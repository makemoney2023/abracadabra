How to use AI in a business is a job list, not a model list. Pick a job you already do, write the steps, and keep a person on the output. The model is the last decision, and for plenty of jobs it is a small one. Statistics Canada's 2025 report asked businesses that already use AI which tools they use and what they changed.[^statcan] Among those businesses, text analytics accounted for 35.7%, and marketing automation for 23.1%. 40.1% had developed new workflows, and 38.9% had trained staff. In the same period, 12.2% of businesses used AI to produce goods or deliver services, up from 6.1% a year earlier. Those are descriptions of what firms reported. They are not a shopping list, and they are not permission to open twelve tools on Monday.

The [help note](/blog/how-can-ai-help-small-businesses) separates that production question from generative-AI use. The [automation note](/blog/ai-automation-for-small-business) is how to pick the first job. The [agents note](/blog/ai-agents-for-business) is when the steps branch and a fixed checklist stops being honest. We'd start with one job from the list below. We'd leave the rest in a stack until that one job has a person on it and a week of real use behind it.

## Twelve jobs you can write down

In each job below, AI does the first pass and a person makes the call:

- Draft the customer reply: a person sends it.
- Summarize the meeting: a person accepts the tasks.
- Turn notes into a quote: a person sets the price.
- Sort the inbox: a person decides what is answered.
- Flag the late invoice: a person decides the chase.
- Describe the product: a person approves the page.
- Group support tickets: a person picks the answer.
- Check the inventory exception: a person orders.
- Rewrite the policy in plain language: a person publishes it.
- Compare two supplier notes: a person chooses.
- Draft the weekly report: a person accepts the numbers.
- Prepare the staff answer: a person trains the staff.

Each line has the same shape. A system prepares. A person decides. If you delete the person, you don't have a job. You have a mailbox that hopes. The reply can be drafted from the thread the customer already wrote. Someone who knows that customer still sends it. The meeting summary can list tasks. Someone who was in the room still accepts them, because a model wasn't there to hear the aside that mattered. The quote can be assembled from notes. The price stays with the owner. We keep repeating that shape because owners keep trying to skip the second half. The second half is the business.

The survey shares above are about businesses that already use AI, except the 12.2% figure, which is all businesses in that Canadian survey. Do not treat a text-analytics share as a share of every firm. Text analytics at 35.7% means that share of AI-using businesses, not of the shop on the corner and not of your street. Marketing automation at 23.1% is the same kind of share. The workflow figure and the training figure, 40.1% and 38.9%, are also among businesses that already use AI. They tell you what changers changed. They don't tell you that a firm which has never tried this is behind, and they don't tell you which of the twelve jobs to run first. The production figure is the odd one out, and it is the one we don't want blurred into the others. Using a tool to draft a reply is not the same claim as using AI to produce the goods or deliver the service.

## A worked example

Take a shop we keep using as the walkthrough. Not a case, not a scoreboard. A counter, a back room, and three people. The owner buys and prices. A clerk covers the floor and the inbox. A bookkeeper chases what wasn't paid. Yesterday, in this telling, looks ordinary. A customer emailed about a size. A supplier emailed a new sheet. Two invoices sat past the date the bookkeeper cares about. A meeting with a landlord produced a page of scribbles and no tasks. The product page for a new item is still a placeholder. None of that requires a new company. It requires someone to name which of those is the job for now.

Say they pick the customer reply, because it happens every day and a wrong tone with a regular is expensive in a way no dashboard needs to explain. The clerk drops the thread into a draft. The draft pulls the size the customer already mentioned and the line the owner uses for delays. The clerk reads it, changes one sentence because this customer hates cheerfulness, and sends it. The system didn't know about the cheerfulness. The clerk did. That is the whole design. Sort the inbox in the same pass if you want, but a person still decides what is answered today and what can wait until tomorrow. Flag the late invoice on a list the bookkeeper already trusts. The bookkeeper decides the chase. A stiff note to a good account is a judgment. A reminder to a stranger who is sixty days quiet is closer to a rule, and even then we like a person to glance.

The quote job shows up the day the shop does custom work. Notes from a conversation become a description. The owner sets the price. We've watched owners try to let the draft set the price because the draft sounded confident. Confidence isn't a cost. The supplier comparison is the same trap with a different costume. Two notes, two minimums, two lead times written in different formats. A draft can line them up. The owner chooses. Choosing is the job. Lining up is the chore.

A clinic can run the same list without pretending to be a shop. Summarize the meeting, then the practitioner accepts the tasks. Prepare the staff answer, then a person trains the staff. Rewrite the policy in plain language, then a person publishes it. The publish step matters because a policy that sounds friendly and says the wrong clinical thing is worse than a stiff policy that is right. A trades desk lives in the quote line and the inventory exception. The lead tech's note becomes a description. The owner prices. The exception gets checked. A person orders. We are not saying every firm should run all twelve. We are saying each of the twelve is a job with an edge, and the edge is a person.

## How we'd run it

First, write the twelve down where the owner can see them, in the firm's own words. "Flag the late invoice" might mean "the Tuesday list the bookkeeper already prints." Use their name for it. A job you can't say in their language isn't a job you can hand over.

Second, pick one. Use a boring test. It happens often, the output is easy to check, and a mistake is visible before a customer is hurt. The customer reply and the late-invoice flag usually pass that test. The weekly report often fails it, because a wrong number becomes "the record" if nobody is assigned to accept it. Draft the weekly report, then a person accepts the numbers. If you can't name that person, don't start there.

Third, write the steps of that one job the way it happened yesterday, not the way the software brochure describes it. Who receives the message. Who is allowed to send. What "done" looks like. Where the draft waits. If the draft doesn't wait, you skipped the design.

Fourth, run it on live work with the old way still allowed. The clerk can still type a reply from scratch. You're watching whether the draft is useful enough that they stop wanting to. If they keep typing from scratch, the draft is wrong, or the job was the wrong one. Fix that before you add a second job.

Fifth, name the person who checks, in a sentence another employee could read. "The clerk sends customer replies. The owner sends anything with a price or an apology." That sentence will save you more pain than a new model. Group support tickets the same way: the system groups, a person picks the answer. Describe the product the same way: the system drafts, a person approves the page.

Sixth, only then look at a second job. The survey pattern we trust isn't the tool share. It's the pair of follow-on moves: new workflows, and staff who were actually trained. 40.1% and 38.9% are shares among firms already using AI, and even there they aren't everyone. A second job without a workflow and without a person who knows it is how the first job dies. Leave the stack alone until the first job is dull.

## Common mistakes

Starting from a model list is the mistake the title is about. Someone asks which chatbot to buy before they can say which reply they want drafted. The tools will multiply. The jobs won't get clearer. We've sat at tables where three logins were already paid for and nobody could point at a sent reply that had passed through any of them. The money wasn't large. The confusion was.

Treating the survey as a ranking of your firm is the next one. A text-analytics share among AI users is not a grade. A production share across all businesses is a different question, and it is smaller for a reason. Most of the help available to a small firm is still a draft, a sort, a summary. That's allowed. Calling it "AI in the product" when it isn't will make you chase a build you don't need. The help note walks that distinction. Use it before you scare yourself with the smaller figure or flatter yourself with the larger one.

Skipping the person on the output is the mistake that reaches the customer. A drafted reply that goes out alone will eventually be warm to the wrong person, or sure about a price the owner never set. A weekly report that nobody accepts will be quoted in a meeting as if it were the books. A policy rewritten into plain language and published unread can change what staff think the rule is. The second half of every line in the list is there because we have seen the first half travel alone.

Running all twelve at once is impatience dressed up as ambition. The stack exists so you don't. A shop that tries replies, invoices, inventory, the product page, and the staff answer in the same month will do all of them badly and then conclude the tools don't work. They weren't given a job. They were given a pile. Pick one. Let it become boring. Boring is the success condition.

Forgetting training is how the workflow figure and the real room come apart. A new workflow that lives in the owner's head is the old business with an extra tab. Show the clerk on a real thread. Show the bookkeeper on a real late invoice. If the only person who can run it is the person who set it up, you don't have a job. You have a hobby.

## What we'd do

We'd pick the customer reply or the late invoice and refuse every other job for a while. That's the opinion. Twelve examples are still a list. A list doesn't run the counter. One checked job does. We'd write the steps in the clerk's words, put their name on the send, and sit with them once on real mail before we called it installed.

We wouldn't buy a second tool to celebrate the first week. We wouldn't turn the quote price over to a draft because the sentences looked finished. We wouldn't publish a rewritten policy because it sounded friendly. And we wouldn't read 35.7% as a target for our shop. It's a share of a different population, in a Canadian survey, among firms that already use AI. Useful as a map of what those firms touch. Useless as a quota.

If you do nothing else after reading this, circle one line from the twelve and write the name of the person who finishes it. If you can't circle one, you don't have a use yet. You have curiosity, which is a fine place to start and a bad thing to automate. The production question, the one about goods and services, can wait until a job you already do has a person on the output and a path someone else could follow.
