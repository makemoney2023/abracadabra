Generative engine optimization and SEO are the same craft in Google's guide, and the page still has to be worth reading by a person. The argument that you must pick a side is a sales argument. It is not how the guide is written.

Google says answer-engine optimization and generative-engine optimization are still SEO.[^google] The page must be indexed and eligible for a snippet. Google does not use llms.txt. There is no special chunking requirement. We keep those lines together because owners are being told to fund a second program beside the first. The guide does not describe a second program. It describes the page you already owed the reader, now also being quoted inside an answer.

The [visibility guide](https://check.abra-ca-dabra.app/check/guide/visibility) is the check for whether that page can be found and cited. The [answer-engine note](/blog/answer-engine-optimization) is the tactic list if you want the methods in one place. The [llms.txt note](/blog/what-is-llms-txt) is the file Google says it ignores, which is worth knowing before someone sells it as the difference between the two names.

## What changes, and what does not

The 2024 paper reports visibility gains of up to 40% when the page is written for a generative answer.[^geo] Citations, quotations, and statistics were associated with gains over 40%. Perplexity showed gains up to 37%. Position-adjusted word count rose 41%, and subjective impression rose 28%, on a benchmark of 10,000 queries. What changes, in that study, is how visible a source becomes inside the generated answer when the writing carries evidence. What does not change is the need for a real page, on a real site, that a crawler can index and a person can use.

Hold the two layers apart when a proposal blurs them. SEO, in the sense Google is using, gets the page indexed and eligible to be quoted as a snippet. The generative layer is what happens when an answer borrows from that page. You can fail the first layer and never reach the second. You can succeed at being quoted and still fail the business, if the quote satisfied the reader and the visit never happens. Both failures are about the same URL. They are not two departments.

Pew found, in July 2025, that a visit with an AI summary was followed by a click 8% of the time, against 15% with no summary.[^pew] SEO still gets the page indexed. The generative layer still has to be worth the click.

That is the practical split we want owners to remember. The index is the price of admission. The click is the part that pays the chair time, the associate, the front desk. A page written only to be summarized will do the first job and skip the second. A page written only as a keyword stub will do neither well, because a summary has nothing solid to lift and a person has nothing solid to trust.

## A worked example

Walk a small dental clinic, as an example, not as a case with a score. Helen owns it. Imani runs the front desk and already answers the same questions the search box gets. Dr. Shah does the dentistry and will not let the website invent clinical promises. The question they care about is the one new patients ask before they call: what happens at a first visit, does it hurt to ask about a cracked tooth, and will you see someone who is nervous.

Their current page is an SEO page in the old, thin sense. City name, "family dentistry," a list of services, a map. It might be indexed. A snippet would be mush. A generated answer, asked about a cracked tooth and a nervous patient, will pass over it for a page that says what the first hour is like, what to bring, and what the clinic will not promise. Helen does not need a second site for "generative" readers. She needs this page to carry both jobs.

What does not change is the floor. The page has to be indexed. It has to be eligible for a snippet. Imani should be able to read the opening lines and say, "Yes, that is what I tell people." Dr. Shah should be able to read the clinical lines and not flinch. If those two cannot sign the page, no engine should be quoting it. Google's refusal of special chunking and of llms.txt belongs here too. Chopping the page into fragments, or adding a side file, does not replace Helen's signature on the sentences.

What changes is the shape of the sentences, and the paper is the reason we say so without turning it into a guarantee. Citations, quotations, and statistics were associated with gains over 40% in that 2024 work, on a benchmark of 10,000 queries. For this clinic the honest version is modest. Quote the after-care instruction you already hand out on paper. Cite the professional guidance you actually follow when you talk about a cracked tooth, instead of a blog that sounds clinical. If you mention a figure, it has to be a figure you can explain to a patient who read it in an answer and walked in holding it. Position-adjusted word count and a subjective impression score moved in the study, 41% and 28%. We do not translate those into a target for Helen. We translate them into "write enough real detail that a reader feels the visit before they book."

The click problem sits on top of that, and Pew is why we will not let the page end at the definition of a cracked tooth. A summary can explain the tooth. The page has to explain Helen's clinic. First visits start with a conversation, not a surprise procedure. Nervous patients can say so when they book, and Imani will leave a longer chair. The clinic does not do every specialty, and it will say which ones it refers. Those lines are useless to a summary that only wanted a definition, and they are the reason a person still taps through. When a summary is on the page, the click showed up 8% of the time in Pew's July 2025 read, against 15% when no summary appeared. Write the lines the summary cannot satisfy.

Roles again, because this is where projects get sloppy. Helen approves what the clinic claims. Imani checks the practical lines against the phone script, including the referral list. Dr. Shah owns anything that could be heard as a diagnosis or a promise of no pain. A freelancer can tidy the headings. A freelancer cannot decide the referral list. If the generative work and the SEO work are billed as two projects, you will get two drafts that disagree, and the patient will meet the disagreement at the desk.

## How we'd run it

We would run one page, once, for both names.

First, pick the question Imani answers every week, and only that question. A first visit. A cracked tooth. What you do with a nervous patient. One page per question. A page that tries to be the whole clinic will be quoted for nothing in particular.

Second, make the SEO floor obvious. Indexed, reachable without a gate, eligible for a snippet, opening lines that answer the question in sentences. If a snippet of those lines would embarrass Dr. Shah, they are the wrong lines. Fix them before you worry about any generative layer.

Third, add evidence the way the 2024 paper describes it, and only evidence you have. A citation you would show a patient. A quotation that is actually yours, or actually the patient's instructions you already print. A statistic you can defend. The study saw gains of up to 40% when pages were written for a generative answer, and gains over 40% tied to citations, quotations, and statistics. Perplexity's reported gains went up to 37%. Use the method on your own material. Do not paste the study's percentages onto the clinic.

Fourth, write the click. Say what the summary will not know. How booking works with Imani. What to bring. What you refuse. Which fear you will take seriously. This is the reply to Pew. Being the source is not the same as being visited.

Fifth, refuse the split invoice. Do not hire one vendor for SEO and another for generative engine work on the same URL unless they edit one document. Two strategies produce two tones, and the answer will quote whichever is cleaner, which may be the one Dr. Shah never saw.

Sixth, read it the way the desk will hear it. Imani reads the practical half aloud. Dr. Shah reads the clinical half. Helen asks, "If this is the only paragraph a summary keeps, is it still true?" If the answer is no, cut until it is. Then publish the one page. The names GEO and SEO can both point at it. They do not each get a website.

## Common mistakes

The first mistake is believing the names are rival crafts. Google says they are still SEO. A clinic that pauses ordinary indexing work to "move to GEO" has misunderstood the guide. The page that is not indexed is not going to be the cited source.

The second mistake is the side file. Google does not use llms.txt for this. Buying a file, or a service that starts with the file, while the first-visit page stays vague, spends the week on the part the guide shrugs off.

The third mistake is special structure for its own sake. There is no special chunking requirement. Headings that match real questions help humans and give an answer a clean edge to lift. Headings that exist only as bait, with nothing under them, help no one. We would rather see four plain sections a patient can scan than a pile of fragments.

The fourth mistake is importing the study as a forecast for your city. Up to 40%, over 40% with citations, quotations, and statistics, up to 37% on Perplexity, word count up 41%, impression up 28%, benchmark of 10,000 queries. That is what the paper measured. It is not Helen's pipeline. Quoting it on the homepage would be the wrong kind of statistic.

The fifth mistake is ignoring the click. Pew's 8% with a summary, against 15% without, is the business problem hiding inside the visibility win. If you only chase the citation, you can "win" a mention that replaces the visit. Put the un-summarizable detail on the page. The person, the refusal, the way booking actually feels.

The sixth mistake is two owners for one page. A marketing seat edits for keywords. Another edits for answers. Neither sits with Imani. The patient meets the gap. One page, one checker at the desk, one clinician who can veto a line.

## What we'd do

We would keep a single page and a single editor, and we would fire the framing that GEO replaces SEO. Our opinion is that the guide is right and the category fight is not. Write the page so a person can use it. That is what makes it indexable, snippet-ready, and worth citing. The 2024 paper tells you that evidence on the page is associated with more visibility inside generated answers. Pew tells you the click gets harder once a summary appears. Those two facts pull in the same direction. Be specific enough to be quoted, and specific in a way the quote cannot finish.

We would spend Helen's money on Imani's questions and Dr. Shah's veto, not on a second strategy deck. If a vendor cannot show the same URL doing both jobs, they are selling a conflict Google did not ask for. The page still has to exist for the list of links and for the cited answer. One page. Make it true, then make it useful, then leave it where a crawler and a nervous patient can both find it.
