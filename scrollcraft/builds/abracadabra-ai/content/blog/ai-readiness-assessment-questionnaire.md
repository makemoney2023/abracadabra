An AI readiness questionnaire should force four themes into the open before anyone names a product. The themes are where the data lives, whether the process is written, who checks the output, and what decision success means. A form that opens with a logo has already skipped the work.

NIST groups the risk work as Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage.[^nist] We are not asking a small firm to stand up a risk program with those labels on the door. We are asking the questionnaire to cover the same ground in plain speech. Govern is who is allowed to decide. Map is what the job actually is. Measure is how you would know the output is wrong. Manage is what you do when it is. A questionnaire that cannot find those four ideas, even in simpler words, is a sales form.

The [framework](/blog/ai-readiness-framework) is how to score the answers. The [assessment](/blog/ai-readiness-assessment) is the longer reading. The [growth guide](https://check.abra-ca-dabra.app/check/guide/growth) is what the check does after the score. Themes come first. A band comes after. A tool comes last, and sometimes it does not come at all.

We like a short questionnaire. Length feels thorough and often hides the blank. If an owner can answer a long run of soft questions and still not say where the record lives, the form dodged the only question that mattered. We would rather four blunt questions that can be answered "we do not know."

## What a public checklist does not claim

The AWS checklist for small and midsize businesses, updated in September 2025, walks an owner through readiness questions.[^aws] It does not publish a percentage of businesses that are ready. The OECD SME AI adoption tool, current in 2026, is a self-assessment aid for smaller firms.[^oecd] It is not a national adoption rate.

That absence is the point of this section, so we will not rush past it. Owners arrive having read a headline that sounds like a share of firms. They want their questionnaire to end in the same kind of share, so they can tell a partner they are above it or below it. Neither of those public pages will give you that share. AWS gives you questions and a way to talk about stages of work. The OECD tool gives a smaller firm a self-check. If your questionnaire prints a readiness rate for "businesses like yours" at the bottom, ask who was counted. If the answer is nobody, you are holding a costume.

We still use those pages. We use them as question prompts, not as a league table. The AWS questions are a decent prod for an owner who has only thought about features. The OECD aid is a decent prod for a smaller firm that does not want an account, a stored answer, or a national story. What you leave with is your own answers. What you do not leave with is permission to say a percentage of your industry is ready. We will not write that sentence for you, because the sources will not write it either.

Ask where the record of truth lives. Ask which step is written and which step is habit. Ask who can reject the output. Ask what you will stop doing if the answer is weak. Those four questions are the questionnaire.

Say them in the language of the job, or they stay abstract. "Where does the record live?" becomes "When a customer calls about the thing we promised, which page does the person on the phone open?" "Which step is habit?" becomes "What do we do when the usual person is out, and is that written anywhere a new hire could find?" "Who can reject the output?" becomes "Who is allowed to say this message does not go to the customer?" "What will we stop?" becomes "If this works, which notebook closes?"

A questionnaire that cannot survive that translation is still a theme list. Themes are not a score. They are the questions you refuse to skip. The growth guide is where we send people once a real score exists and the next move is about the business getting better at the job, not about collecting more questions. Do not open that guide to avoid the four questions. Open it after you have answers you can stand behind.

<!--image:3-->

We also want the questionnaire to record disagreement. If the owner and the person who does the job give different answers, the form should show both, not a negotiated average. The average is how a blank becomes a tick. Write "owner says the binder, dispatcher says her head" and leave it ugly. Ugly is honest. A clean tick that required a compromise in the hallway is how you buy a tool for a process that does not exist.

NIST's four words help us check that we did not drift into product trivia. If the questionnaire never asks who decides, it skipped Govern. If it never asks what the job is in steps a stranger could follow, it skipped Map. If it never asks how you would notice a wrong output, it skipped Measure. If it never asks what you will do when the output is wrong, it skipped Manage. You can fail those tests with a very professional-looking form. We have watched it happen. The form had sections and a logo and nowhere to write "we do not know."

## A worked example

Say we are at the desk of a small electrical contractor, late afternoon, phones finally quiet. There is an owner, a dispatcher, and a lead who actually walks the jobs. This is an example we walk through. It is not a firm we audited, and we are not going to pretend we counted their jobs.

The owner was handed a questionnaire by a software seller. It opened with the product name and asked how many users she might want, whether she liked dashboards, and how soon she could start. None of that is a readiness question. We set it aside and ask our four.

Where does the record of truth live for a booked job? The owner says the job system. The dispatcher says he keeps a whiteboard because the system is wrong by the time a customer changes a time. The lead says she trusts the text the dispatcher sends her in the morning, because the whiteboard and the system have both lied. Three answers. The questionnaire does not get to average them. The record of truth is unresolved, and that is the answer we write.

Which step is written, and which step is habit? The written step is the quote. The owner is proud of the quote template, and she should be. The habit is everything after the customer says yes. Who tells the lead the parking note? Who calls the customer when the part slips? Who decides a job is done enough to invoice? The dispatcher says "we just do it." The lead says she calls the owner when she is unsure, which means the owner's phone is the process. A step that lives in a phone is not a written step. We mark it as habit.

Who can reject the output? This is the question that makes the room go quiet, in a good way. If a tool drafted the morning text to the lead, who could say it is wrong and stop it? The dispatcher assumes he would notice. The lead says she would notice on site, which is late. The owner says she would want to approve messages at first, then admits she will not read them once the week gets loud. So nobody currently has a real reject button. The people theme is blank. We do not fill it with optimism.

What will they stop doing if the answer is weak? The owner wants to stop repeating the same parking notes. That is a real stop. The dispatcher wants to stop rebuilding the day from scratch each morning. Also real. The lead wants to stop arriving without the part. If the questionnaire cannot connect a tool to one of those stops, the tool is a hobby. We write the stop next to the blank, so the blank has a cost they can feel.

We do not end this example with a score. Themes are not a score. The [framework](/blog/ai-readiness-framework) is where those answers get words like absent or partial. The [assessment](/blog/ai-readiness-assessment) is where a band gets explained. What this conversation produced is a form with blanks the owner cannot talk her way out of. That is a finished questionnaire. A finished sale would have been a different meeting, and a worse one.

## How we'd run it

First we throw out any question that starts with a product. If the sentence can be answered by a vendor's brochure, it is not on our form. The owner's seller asked about dashboards. Dashboards are a feature. Features are what you discuss after the themes, and only if the themes survived.

Second we ask the four questions in job language, one job only. At this desk the job is a booked call from the yes to the lead packing the van. We do not questionnaire the whole company. A form about "the business" produces slogans. A form about one job produces places, names, and blanks.

Third we ask each person separately before we let them repair each other's answers. The dispatcher will defer to the owner if she is in the room from the start. The lead will go quiet if the form starts to sound like a test of her. We want the first answer, the one they would give a new hire. Then we put the answers side by side and let the conflict stand.

Fourth we map the answers onto Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage without making anyone say those words out loud. Who decides is Govern. The steps of the booked job are Map. How the lead would know the morning text is wrong is Measure. What the dispatcher does when it is wrong is Manage. If one of those is missing, the questionnaire is not done, no matter how many other boxes are ticked.

Fifth we decide whether a score is even useful yet. If the record of truth is still three answers, a band will only decorate the confusion. We stay with the questions. If the four themes have answers we can point at, we move to the check and then to the growth guide, which is about what to do after you know the state. The guide is not a way to skip the form.

We keep the session short enough that the lead is not late to a job and the dispatcher is not dispatching from the hallway. A questionnaire completed by the owner alone on a Sunday night will be complete and fictional. The person who does the step has to be in the room.

## Common mistakes

The mistake we see most is starting with the tool. The seller's form feels efficient because it ends in a quote. Efficiency here is how you skip the record, the habit, and the person who can say no. You will pay for that skip monthly.

Another mistake is treating a public checklist as a readiness rate. The AWS page, updated in September 2025, does not publish a percentage of businesses that are ready. The OECD aid, current in 2026, is not a national adoption rate. If your notes say "we are ahead of most firms," you wrote a sentence your sources refused to write.

A third mistake is smoothing disagreement. When the owner, the dispatcher, and the lead name different records, the honest questionnaire shows the split. A single tick marked "system of record: yes" is a fiction they agreed to so the meeting could end. The meeting ending is not the goal. The blank is the goal.

We also see forms that ask about attitude. "How excited is your team about AI?" is not a theme. Excitement does not locate the record. A skeptical the lead who can reject a bad text is more ready than a cheerful room with no reject button. Ask about permission, not mood.

The last mistake is a questionnaire with no "stop doing" line. If nothing will be stopped, nothing was decided. You gathered opinions. You did not assess the job.

## What we'd do

We would use a one-page form with those four questions and a place to write two names when the answers conflict. Our opinion is that a long assessment questionnaire is often a way to feel serious without naming the whiteboard. The owner does not need more questions. She needs the dispatcher's answer and the lead's answer on the same sheet, in ink, where a dashboard question used to be.

If we were sitting with them next week, we would not bring a tool. We would bring the four questions again and see if the record of truth has become one place. If it has, we would score it. If it has not, we would still not buy. A questionnaire that cannot produce a no is not an assessment. It is a path to a signature.
