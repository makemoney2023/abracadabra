Hire a consultant to name the job, and hire an agency only after that naming is done. An AI consultant names the job, the data, and the stop. An automation agency builds the system after that naming is done. Hiring the agency first produces a build with no owner. We've watched that sequence in the wrong order more than once. The proposal arrives with staff, a toolset, and a start date. Nobody in the room can say which output a person still has to check. The build proceeds anyway, because the calendar was the only decision anyone made.

The [consultant note](/blog/ai-consultant-for-small-business) is the question list we wish owners had in their pocket before the first sales call. The [buy-or-build note](/blog/off-the-shelf-ai-or-custom-software) is whether to build at all. The [decision guide](https://check.abra-ca-dabra.app/check/guide/fix/ready-decision-mid) is the matching score when the choice itself is the thing you're avoiding. Hiring is a decision. Score it before you sign. A signature on a vague scope is not a strategy. It's a hope with a payment schedule.

## Two training figures, two populations

Microsoft's 2024 index says only 25% of companies were planning to offer generative AI training.[^microsoft] Statistics Canada's 2025 report says 38.9% of Canadian businesses that already use AI had trained staff.[^statcan] Those are different populations. One is a plan among companies. The other is training among businesses that already use AI. Do not average them. A pitch that slides the two numbers into "about a third of firms train people" has invented a fact. The plan figure is about companies that were intending to offer training. The Canadian figure is about businesses already using AI that had actually trained staff. Intending and having done it are different. Companies and AI-using businesses are different. Countries and survey windows are different. If a consultant or an agency treats them as one knob, they are not reading. They are decorating.

<!--image:2-->

McKinsey's 2025 survey says 23% are scaling an agentic system somewhere, and 39% are experimenting.[^mckinsey] An agency pitch that quotes only a use figure is selling the experiment. Experimenting is the larger group. Scaling somewhere is the smaller one, and "somewhere" matters. Somewhere is not "in the job you care about," and it is not "in a firm the size of yours." We say this because the word agent shows up in proposals before anyone has admitted they are still in the experiment. An experiment can be the right buy if it is labeled as one, with a stop and a person. An experiment sold as a finished operation is how the owner ends up maintaining a demo.

<!--cta:inline-->

Pay a consultant to refuse the work that a product already does. Pay an agency when the process is written and the checker is named. The refusal is the valuable part, and it's the part a build contract is bad at. A builder is paid to build. A namer can be paid to say "don't." If you only hire people who are paid when the project starts, you will get a project. You may not get a job worth doing.

## A worked example

Take a clinic that almost hired an agency to "put an agent on the front desk." This is a walkthrough, not a scoreboard. The owner had three calls in a week and a folder of proposals. The front desk still books by phone and by a form on the site. The practitioner still decides which visits are in scope. Billing still waits for a note that the visit happened. Ask what the agent would be allowed to send and the room goes quiet. Ask what happens when the draft is wrong and the room gets quieter. That quiet is the consultant's whole engagement. Name the job. Name the data the job is allowed to see. Name the stop.

In that room we'd send them to a consultant before any build. The consultant's output is a page, not a login. The page might say: the front desk drafts reminders, a person sends them, the practitioner is the stop when the reminder includes a clinical instruction, and billing is not in this job at all. It might also say the booking product they already pay for sends a plain reminder, so don't build one. That refusal is worth more than a sprint. An agency that starts before the page exists will build the reminder anyway, because a reminder is easy to demo. Easy to demo is not the same as missing.

A trades desk is the other shape, and it's where an agency can be the right call. The quote steps are already written. The office manager logs the address. The lead tech files measurements before leaving the driveway. The owner prices, and the price is the stop. The bookkeeper doesn't open an invoice until the yes is written down. A product they tried doesn't hold site photos and supplier notes in one place. The checker has a name. That's a build. Even then we'd want the agency to repeat the stop back to us before they estimate. If they talk about agents and never say the owner's name next to the price, they're selling the experiment McKinsey's larger share is still in.

A shop is usually neither a big consulting saga nor a custom build. The owner wants someone to "do AI" because a neighbor did. The clerk already knows the reorder. The bookkeeper already has a late-invoice list. A consultant who earns the fee will point at one of those, refuse the rest, and go home. An agency that earns the fee will only show up if that one job truly needs a system no product covers. Most weeks, it doesn't. Paying either of them to rediscover the clerk's list is a polite way to avoid deciding.

## How we'd run it

First, write the job in one sentence a new hire could read. Not "AI for the front desk." The front desk sends appointment reminders after a person reads them. If you can't get to a sentence like that, you are hiring a mood. Moods are expensive when they come with a team.

Second, write the data the job may touch and the data it may not. Reminders can see the time and the place. They can't see the clinical note unless a named practitioner says so. Quotes can see the site measurements. They can't invent a price. If the data line is "whatever the model needs," you don't have a boundary. You have a hope that the vendor will be careful. Vendors are careful about their scope. You have to be careful about yours.

Third, name the checker in the same sentence as the job. A person, a place where the output waits, and what happens if it's wrong. A checkpoint nobody owns is not a checkpoint. We'd rather delay the hire than sign a scope where "someone will review" is the only human. Someone is not a name. Everyone goes home.

Fourth, ask whether a product already does this. The consultant is paid to refuse that work. If the booking tool already sends the plain reminder, stop. If the books already flag the late invoice, stop. Bring an agency in only for the gap that remains after that refusal. The buy-or-build note is the longer version of this step. Don't skip it because an agency proposal is already pretty.

Fifth, separate the training story from the build story. The 25% figure is companies planning to offer training. The 38.9% figure is AI-using businesses that had trained staff. Neither figure is a promise that your clerk will be shown the path. Put the showing in the scope. A launch without a person who can run the job next Tuesday is a launch into the owner's memory, which is where the work already lived.

Sixth, if the proposal mentions agents, ask whether they are scaling or experimenting, and make them say which. 23% and 39% are not invitations. They're a warning about language. If the honest answer is experiment, write "experiment" at the top of the scope, with a date you'll decide to keep it or turn it off. If they can't say, you're buying a word.

## Common mistakes

Hiring the agency because the proposal had more pages is the mistake with the nicest stationery. Pages are not a named job. A bench of people is not a checker. We've sat with owners who chose the thicker folder because it felt like seriousness. Seriousness is a person who will tell you not to start. The thinner conversation is often the one that saves the quarter. We don't mean a cheap conversation. We mean a conversation that is allowed to end in no.

Averaging the training figures is the mistake that makes everyone sound informed. Someone says firms don't train, and someone else quotes the other survey as if it were the same study, and the room nods. They aren't. One is a plan in a 2024 index of companies. One is training already done among Canadian businesses that use AI, in a 2025 report. Use either number alone, with its population, or use neither. The fake average will be repeated to your staff as if it were a law. It isn't.

Buying the experiment under a scaling headline is how agent language gets into a clinic that needed a reminder. McKinsey's split is the antidote if you actually say it out loud in the meeting. Most of the respondents in that survey were not scaling. If your seller only quotes a use number, ask what happened to the people who were still experimenting. Then ask which group the proposal belongs to. Silence is an answer. Treat it as one.

Leaving the refusal out of the contract is how you pay twice. You pay the agency to build the thing the product already did, and you pay the product to keep doing it. A consultant who won't refuse is a salesperson with a notebook. We'd rather pay for a no on a single page than pay for a yes that duplicates the booking tool. Put the right to stop in writing before the build has a channel and a standup.

Skipping the owner of the output is the last one, and it survives all the others. The scope names a platform. It doesn't name the office manager. On the first real day the draft is ready and nobody's job includes looking at it. The agency did what it was paid to do. The consultant, if you had one, either wasn't listened to or wasn't asked. The draft waits in a queue that is technically a success. Customers never see a success. They see whatever escaped.

## What we'd do

We'd hire the namer first, almost every time, and we'd pay them to be willing to end the engagement with a refusal. That's the opinion. A clinic that can't say what the front desk is allowed to send doesn't need an agency yet. A shop whose late invoices already live in the bookkeeping product doesn't need either of them. A trades desk with the steps written, the price owned by a named person, and a gap no product covers is the rare room where we'd call a builder. Even there, the builder doesn't get to rename the job.

We wouldn't let a single training statistic into the proposal without its population sitting next to it. We wouldn't let "agent" into the scope unless someone can show the step that branches and the person who stops it. We wouldn't sign a start date that arrives before the page. Dates are easy. Pages are the work. If the seller needs the date to hold their staff together, that is their problem to solve after you know what you're buying.

If you only do one thing before the next call, write the sentence with the job and the checker, and ask the seller to repeat it without adding a platform. If they can't, you are not late. You are early, which is the correct time to be early. The experiment will still be there next month. Your name on a vague scope is harder to get back.
