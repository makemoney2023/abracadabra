An AI implementation framework is the order of work you follow once the job is already named. NIST calls the risk side of that work Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage.[^nist] We use five stages in plain words, because that is how the conversation actually goes when we sit with an owner. Name the job. Score the data. Write the process. Assign a checker. Then keep the work or stop it.

Most small firms we meet are still in the [Forming band](https://check.abra-ca-dabra.app/band/forming) while those stages are unfinished. A tool is open on someone's laptop. The rest is a hope. Hope is not a stage. If you skip one, you get the stall we describe in the [stall note](/blog/why-ai-pilots-stall). The score that should come before any build is the [readiness framework](/blog/ai-readiness-framework). This note is only the order of work after that score exists.

We like a boring order. Owners like a demo. The demo feels like progress because someone in the room relaxes. The order feels like delay because it is a list. The list is the work. A framework that cannot be walked on a single page is a brochure, and we will not pretend a brochure runs on Monday.

## What the numbers say about the middle

McKinsey's 2025 survey says 88% report regular AI use in at least one function.[^mckinsey] That is the easy number, and it is the one a vendor will quote before the coffee arrives. The harder numbers sit beside it. 23% are scaling an agentic system somewhere, and 39% are experimenting. 29% of firms under $100 million are scaling, against nearly half of firms over $5 billion. No more than 10% scale agents in any one function.

Read that last line slowly if you run a shop, a clinic, or a trades desk. Regular use is a person opening a tool. Scaling inside one function means the function runs on a written process, with a person who can stop it. The gap between those two is where projects live as a browser tab nobody closes. We do not think a small firm is behind because it is not among the firms scaling an agentic system. We think it is on time if it can finish one function without lying about who checks the output.

<!--image:2-->

Cisco's 2025 survey of 8,000 leaders found pacesetters, near 13% of that sample, were 4 times more likely to move a pilot into production.[^cisco] Those leaders work in organizations with more than 500 employees. A small business can still copy the sequence those pacesetters used. Write the process, then put it into production. You cannot copy their headcount, their internal bench, or the quiet fact that a pilot in that survey is not the same object as a receptionist trying a new inbox on a Tuesday. Take the sequence. Leave the costume.

<!--cta:inline-->

Stop at the stage you cannot finish. A checker you cannot name means you are not ready to keep the work. We would rather you pause on a named page than push a half-built flow into the week the office manager is already losing to the phones.

## A worked example

Take a neighborhood dental clinic. This is a walk-through we use when an owner asks what the stages look like on a real week. It is not a result we measured. The owner is the dentist, and she still does the exams. The office manager runs the schedule, the billing questions, and the front desk when someone calls in sick. The front-desk lead answers the phone and sends reminders. A hygienist knows which patients are midway through treatment and which ones simply need a cleaning.

The job is narrow. Patients who are due for a cleaning get a short reminder that sounds like the clinic. The dentist does not want a bot booking anything clinical, and she does not want a guess at a balance. She wants the recall message, and she wants a person to see it before it leaves.

We write the job where the front-desk lead can repeat it. Draft the cleaning reminder from the appointment record. Do not mention treatment. Do not offer a price. A person sends it. If a second job is hiding in that sentence, we start again. A seller will try to widen it in the proposal. The sentence is how we say no.

The data gets scored before anyone connects an account. The appointment book lives in the practice software. A paper list in the manager's drawer names people who asked not to be texted. Insurance notes sit on another screen. The hygienist knows which patients are angry about a bill. Those sources disagree. A draft built only from the appointment book will write to someone on the paper list. That is the clinic telling the truth about its files, and it shows up on the first Monday if anyone looks.

The process is a sequence of hands. Monday morning the front-desk lead pulls the due list. The draft uses the appointment record only. Anything that smells like a treatment word goes to the hygienist. The office manager reads the rest and sends it. If she is out, the queue waits. Nobody sends it because the draft looked sure of itself.

The checker is the office manager, named, with time held on the calendar. If she cannot do it, the hygienist is the named backup. "Whoever is free" is not a checker.

After a run of real Mondays they look at what the manager rejected. Wording problems mean they keep the job and tighten the line. Wrong people mean they stop sending and fix the paper list and the appointment book. Stopping is a finished stage. The demo took an afternoon. The paper list and the rule about waiting took the month.

## How we'd run it

We run the stages in order. We do not open the next one while the current one is still a shrug.

First, we sit with the owner and with one person who does the job by hand. We ask to see last week's work on the screen they actually use. We write one sentence that names the job, the input, and the thing the tool must not touch. If they want several jobs, we pick the one that happens every week and hurts when it is late. The rest stay on a closed list. Opening that list is how a reminder becomes "the whole patient experience" before anyone has sent a real one.

Second, we score the data in the room. Where does the record live, who types it, what happens when it is wrong, and which other list contradicts it. In the clinic that is the appointment book against the do-not-text drawer. On a trades desk it is the job ticket against the text thread where the customer changed the fixture. In a shop it is the register against the note scribbled on the bag. If nobody can point at the source, we stop. We will not agree to clean it later. Later is how bad names get a green check.

Third, we write a short script for a loud morning. Who pulls the list. What the draft may see. Who reads it. What a strange one does. What happens when that person is out. We read it back to the person who will run it and we change it until they say they would follow it when the phones are bad. A script they will not follow is decoration.

Fourth, we name the checker and we hold the time on the calendar before anything is switched on. We also write where a rejection lands. A side chat does not count. If the reject is invisible, the same mistake returns and everyone treats it as new.

Fifth, we keep or stop, using a rule written before the first live send. Keep means the checker can clear the queue in the time we held, and the rejects are about wording. Stop means we unplug the send step and go back to the files. We do not add another job to soften the stop. We do not let a seller rename the sentence as the opening of a platform.

We stay with the owner through this, not only with the person who bought the license. The owner is who a friend will text. Our part is to read the original sentence again.

## Common mistakes

Starting with the tool is the one we see most. A cousin sends a link, and the first hour is logins. The job is still fog. Connecting an account is easy. You cannot quietly undo messages sent to people who asked to be left alone.

The demo is the next trap. The records on screen are clean because someone prepared them. The do-not-text list is missing. So is the Monday when two people are out. Signing while the sample is still up means you bought a performance.

A checker on a slide and not on a calendar will rot the queue. "The office will review" is not a name. We have watched reminders leave because each person assumed another had looked. The patient hears about a cleaning while they are already in the chair. The apology lands on the front desk, who never agreed to catch it.

Widening a quiet job is how a sensible project turns sour. The reminder feels calm, so someone points the same login at billing. A clumsy reminder is an awkward call. A wrong balance is a trust problem. They do not belong together because they share a password.

Copying a big-company rollout flatters the room and starves the desk. Cisco's pacesetters were 4 times more likely to move a pilot into production, and they work in organizations with more than 500 employees. They have people whose job is the process. You have a manager who also covers lunch. Copy the sequence. Do not copy a stack of pilots.

The emotional mistake is refusing to stop. A stop feels like a public failure, so the limp workflow stays alive. A stopped send, with the reason on the same page as the job, means the framework worked. A tab that is always "being tweaked" means it did not.

## What we'd do

We would make the owner pick one job, and we would walk away if they would not. That sounds stern if you wanted a partner who nods. It is how we have seen this work finish. Use is common. Scaling inside one function is rare. No more than 10% scale agents in any one function. The firms in that thin slice finished a process. They did not win a contest for the most logins.

We would not aim a clinic at the Cisco pacesetter share. Near 13% of a sample of 8,000 leaders, in organizations with more than 500 employees, is a different building. The useful part is what they did. They were 4 times more likely to move a pilot into production because the process was written first. A clinic can copy that with a manager and a calendar hold. It cannot copy it by exhausting a part-time desk and calling the exhaustion progress.

We would keep the old reminder running beside the draft until the manager trusts what she reads. Sellers dislike that, because the product looks optional. It is cheaper than a week of apologies. When she trusts it, we turn the old way off in a sentence the staff can hear, so nobody keeps doing both in secret.

If a tool is already on the card statement, do not ask us to make it sound larger than it is. Ask which stage you cannot finish. A fuzzy job goes back to the [readiness framework](/blog/ai-readiness-framework). A charming demo and a bad Monday belong with the [stall note](/blog/why-ai-pilots-stall). A habit that is still forming can sit in the [Forming band](https://check.abra-ca-dabra.app/band/forming) until the checker has a name. That is a respectable place to be.
